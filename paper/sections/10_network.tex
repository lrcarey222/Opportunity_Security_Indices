\section{The Allied Industrial Base: stress tests, partnerships and Sectoral Compacts}
\label{sec:network}

The indices of Sections~\ref{sec:es}--\ref{sec:bilateral} score countries, sectors and pairs one at
a time. The case for an Allied Industrial Base turns on a different question
\citep{neis2026allied}: what happens to the allies' supply when a dominant supplier is lost, how much
of the loss their existing trade relationships can absorb, what coordinated trade adds, and where
only new capacity closes the gap. A top-ranked country may add little if its strengths duplicate the
coalition's, and a lower-ranked one may complete a chain, so the object is a country's contribution
to the system rather than its rank.

This section treats the Allied Industrial Base as an \emph{emergent network} of bilateral trade and
investment relationships, and Sectoral Compacts as the institutions that coordinate groups of those
relationships where joint action materially improves resilience. The analysis is a chain of
explicit steps on the observed 2024 trade in U.S. dollars: a supply shock at each supply-chain node;
the response of the existing relationships; the trade that optimized partnerships could achieve; the
residual gap that trade cannot close; a standardised capacity increment at candidate hosts; the
network of relationships that emerges; and the groups of nodes and countries for which a compact is
worth forming, with a policy treatment for each node. Every step is reported, and no step is a
weighted composite of the others: there is no AIB score. The analysis is conditional and positive.
It shows what the observed network can and cannot do under a stated shock, not where production will
locate, and it prices neither cost nor time to build. Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm} states the
programs and Online Appendix~\ref{oa:network} traces four nodes through every step. The analysis
replaces the allocation of production by a mixed-integer program with a calibrated diversification
penalty that earlier drafts of this paper reported (Appendix~\ref{app:corrections}).

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Nodes, flows and the two sets of economies}
\label{subsec:aib_nodes}

A node is a sub-sector of a technology stage in the HS6 master of Section~\ref{sec:data}: for
example rare-earth compounds, permanent magnets or battery cells. Solar polysilicon and wafers,
one sub-sector in the master, are split on their HS6 codes. The ten technologies with a trade
basket (the nine headline technologies other than Geothermal and Green Hydrogen, plus
Semiconductors) give 92 nodes in their Upstream and Midstream stages; Downstream deployment is left
out. Where several technologies share the same HS6 set (permanent magnets, rare-earth compounds and
rare-earth metals in Electric Vehicles and Wind, transformers in Electric Grid and Solar, pipelines in
Oil and Gas) the cell is one physical node, counted once in network totals. The HS6 codes are the
data, the node is the unit of optimization, the stage is the aggregation and policy frame, and every
metric is recomputed from the flows summed to the node: no HS6-level score is averaged.

Flows are the reconciled 2024 Comtrade dyads of Section~\ref{sec:data}: the importer's declaration
where the importer reports the node, otherwise the exporter's declaration times 1.06
\citep{uncomtrade}. An importer's demand for a node is its observed imports, so domestic production
consumed at home lies outside the analysis and coverage is always coverage of \emph{traded} demand.
Observed exports are read as revealed tradable capability, not as spare physical capacity. Each
node is checked against data-quality thresholds (world trade, the number of material exporters and
importers, the share of mirror flows, codes shared with other nodes, HS revisions, and production
far more concentrated than trade): 23 nodes are of high confidence, 68 usable with caution and one,
cobalt ores and concentrates, too thin to optimize after thin nodes are merged into a sibling linked
by a material flow (monazite into rare-earth compounds). Ninety-one nodes are optimized.

Two sets of economies are kept apart. The \emph{demand scope} is the set of importers whose lost
supply the analysis seeks to restore: the 57 coalition members of Section~\ref{sec:coalition}, 31 core
members and 26 partners, each EU member in its own right. The \emph{candidate partners} are the
exporters that may open, expand or redirect relationships toward the scope: any economy other than
the shocked one, with at least USD 1 million and 0.05 percent of the node's world exports (the larger
bar binds); a median node has 43 candidates (9 to 74). The coalition is thus the set of economies
protected, not a closed supplier set, and a supplier outside it counts wherever it supplies. Open
ship registries (Liberia, the Marshall Islands, Panama and the Bahamas), which trade vessels under
their flags, are in neither set. Compacts and network edges are formed only in the
\emph{electro-industrial} scope, the 70 nodes of Batteries, Electric Vehicles, Solar, Wind, Electric
Grid, Semiconductors and Nuclear; coal, oil and gas nodes are modelled and reported but form no
compact. Which economies are protected, which may supply them and which technologies are strategic
are preferences, and Section~\ref{subsec:robust_network} reports the results under other choices.

\subsection{Shocks, existing relationships and optimized trade}
\label{subsec:aib_trade}

\paragraph{Shocks.} The headline shock removes China's exports in every node, with the re-exports of
Hong Kong and Macao; sensitivities halve them or remove the largest supplier of the sub-sector, the
stage or the technology. Two readings of a shock are kept. Under \emph{hold}, the headline, the
shocked economy keeps buying: the shock is an export restriction, so other exporters' sales to China
stay with China, which competes for the same supply. Under \emph{release}, a decoupling, China's
purchases fall by the same fraction and the exports headed to it are freed for others. Release is the
more optimistic reading wherever third countries sell much to the shocked economy, as Myanmar and Laos
do in rare-earth concentrates and Chile in lithium. Shock exposure is the share of the scope's traded
demand for the node that the shock removes.

\paragraph{Three states.} In state A nothing adapts: the shocked supply disappears. In state B the
surviving candidates may reallocate only through relationships observed in 2024, plus keeping their
own exports at home, and an existing relationship may grow to at most twice its observed size. In
state C, optimized partnerships, any candidate may serve any importer in the scope. A candidate's
available supply is its current shipments to the scope plus, in the \emph{reallocation} scenario of
the headline, its exports to markets outside the scope (under hold, not its sales to China); in the
\emph{conservative} scenario every surviving shipment into the scope stays where it is and only the
supply the shock frees can move. What a candidate draws beyond its own shipments to the scope is
reported as diversion, supply taken from buyers outside it. The bound on state B matters: without
it, a relationship of USD 10{,}000 could carry any volume and existing relationships would be as
flexible as new ones. The difference between the two states,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aib_dividend}
  \text{coordination dividend}_n \;=\; C^{\mathrm C}_n - C^{\mathrm B}_n ,
\end{equation}
where $C_n$ is the share of the scope's traded demand for node $n$ served after the shock, is the
value of new or expanded relationships, that is, of coordination. The residual gap
$1-C^{\mathrm C}_n$ is what trade cannot close.

\paragraph{The program.} States B and C are linear programs over the flows from candidates to
importers, solved lexicographically: each objective is optimized only among the solutions that keep
the earlier ones within a tolerance of their optimum \citep{ehrgott2005multicriteria,makhorin2020glpk}.
The objectives are (1) to maximise demand served; (1b) to cut surviving shipments as little as
possible, so that trade the shock did not touch is not reshuffled; (2) to preserve revealed
specialization, weighting exporters by capped revealed comparative advantage
\citep{balassa1965trade} or by their production share where a proxy exists; (3) to favour
relationships with higher Export Opportunity ($\PO$ of Section~\ref{sec:bilateral}, exporter to
market); and (4) to favour revealed policy and climate compatibility (similarity of IEA policy
intensity in the node's technology and stage and of Climate Action Tracker ratings). The tolerance on
coverage is 0.05 percent, so the later objectives decide only among allocations that serve the same
demand. No geopolitical alignment score enters, and none of the scores is a weight on coverage.
Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm} gives the program.

\paragraph{Physical capability.} Trade values are a poor guide to who can produce where the dominant
producer consumes most of its own output: world trade in polysilicon and wafers is far below world
production, and HS 3818.00, the wafer heading, is mostly semiconductor wafers from Japan, Taiwan and
Korea. In the 11 nodes with production or capacity shares covering at least 80 percent of world
output (IEA mining and refining shares, \citealp{iea2026cmdata}; manufacturing capacity,
\citealp{cim2026}) the headline is \emph{production-constrained}: an exporter the physical data list
as a producer keeps its full trade response, while one they do not list (a re-exporter, stocks or a
different product under the same code) keeps its current shipments to the scope but cannot expand or
redirect toward it. Shares are not multiplied into trade values, since production is a volume and
trade a value. The \emph{trade-only} result, in which exports alone bound supply, is kept beside it.
Where the class of a node rests on low trade exposure while physical and trade concentration diverge
by 25 points or more, it carries the warning \textsc{physical capability not validated}.

\subsection{Results: exposure, coverage and the coordination dividend}
\label{subsec:aib_results}

Removing China's supply costs the 57 economies 14.2 percent of their traded demand across the 91
nodes, USD 756 billion of USD 5{,}326 billion, and 22.9 percent in the electro-industrial scope (USD
702 billion of USD 3{,}063 billion), where coverage falls to 77.1 percent (Figure~\ref{fig:aib_ladder}).
Existing relationships restore it to 84.4 percent and optimized trade to 84.7 percent. Batteries are
most exposed: 47.5 percent of the scope's traded demand is lost and 45.2 percent remains unserved
after optimized trade. Solar (25.6 percent lost, gap 18.1), Electric Vehicles (24.7, 18.7), Wind (22.2,
12.1), Semiconductors (18.1, 6.7), Electric Grid (16.6, 10.3) and Nuclear (8.6, 3.4) follow; coal, gas
and oil lose 2 to 4 percent and close almost all of it. The recovery is not new supply: under hold,
all of it, USD 283 billion across the nodes and USD 232 billion in the electro-industrial scope, is
supply drawn from buyers outside the scope, so the allies' coverage rises because others' falls.

The coordination dividend is close to zero. It is below one point in 81 of the 91 nodes, with a
median of 0.05 points, and reaches the five-point threshold for coordination only in wind towers, at
6.7 points, where existing relationships already restore 93.3 percent (Figure~\ref{fig:aib_nodes}).
The reason is the reach of the existing network: with global candidate partners and existing
relationships allowed to double, the network of 2024 already carries almost everything trade can do,
and what remains is the difference between what the world outside China exports and what the allies
lost. In the most exposed nodes the gap is close to the exposure itself (panel~a): seven nodes lose
more than half their traded supply and recover little of it, battery cells (61.1 percent lost, gap
60.3), anode materials, vehicle batteries and packs, solar modules, permanent magnets, natural graphite
and lithium chemicals. The problem the stress test reveals is capacity, not the coordination of trade.

\subsection{Residual gaps and marginal investment}
\label{subsec:aib_investment}

Only after optimized trade, and only in nodes where at least 5 percent of demand remains unserved,
does the analysis ask where new capacity would help. It is a marginal capacity simulation, not a
project appraisal: a standardised increment of 5, 10 or 20 percent of the node's demand-scope demand
is added at one eligible host at a time and state C re-solved, and the gain in coverage is recorded
with the change in concentration. A host is eligible only with revealed capability at material scale:
revealed comparative advantage of at least one on exports of at least USD 50 million or 1 percent of
world trade in the node, a production share of at least 1 percent, a world export share of at least
0.5 percent, or that comparative advantage in a node joined to it by a material link. Hosts are ranked
by (1) the gain in coverage with a 40 percent supplier-share limit in force, so that capacity at an
already dominant supplier counts for little; (2) the fall in concentration; (3) capability at scale,
the larger of the host's world export and production shares; (4) Friendshore
(Section~\ref{sec:bilateral}) from the importers whose supply was lost; and (5) policy compatibility.
Friendshore orders capable hosts and never admits an incapable one.

In the headline 49 electro-industrial nodes qualify, with 83 distinct eligible hosts and a median of
20 per node. At the 10 percent increment the leading host closes the median gap from 18.7 to 8.7
percent, and closes it fully in 10 nodes; its gain is the full increment in the median node, since one
increment is smaller than most gaps. Most leading hosts qualify on comparative advantage (41 of 49),
five on production shares. Twenty-nine economies lead at least one node: Czechia leads seven (among them
power converters, grid control panels, electrical steel, wind foundations and turbines), Viet Nam five
(solar racking, cabling, module inputs, array cables and integrated circuits), the Philippines three, and
Denmark, Spain, Finland, Indonesia, Korea, Malaysia, Taiwan and the United States two each.

The investor--host pairs follow from the same simulation: with every other flow fixed, the new
capacity at the leading host fills the unmet demand of the importers that lost supply, in descending
order of the host's Export Opportunity toward them. Only the leading (first-ranked) host of each node
enters the flows; the hosts ranked second to fifth are alternatives for the same increment and are
never added to it. Each importer served is an \emph{investor}: the
buyer whose lost supply the new capacity replaces and which therefore has the interest to finance or
contract for it. Across the 49 nodes the leading hosts' capacity supplies USD 178 billion to 57
investors in 618 investor--host pairs, beside USD 29 billion that hosts keep for their own demand
(Figure~\ref{fig:aib_flows}). The largest pairs are Germany with Finland (USD 9.9 billion, in vehicle
batteries and packs), the United States with Hungary (USD 7.4 billion, charging infrastructure), with
the Philippines (USD 6.0 billion in three nodes) and with Taiwan (USD 5.8 billion, battery cells), and
Mexico with Hungary (USD 5.7 billion). Core members host 58.9 percent of the supply, partners 33.6
percent and economies outside the coalition 7.4 percent, chiefly Taiwan. The supply-weighted mean
Friendshore of the investor toward the host is 0.77.

\subsection{The emergent network and country roles}
\label{subsec:aib_network}

The relationships the analysis selects form the emergent network: 2{,}029 directed pairs among 108
economies in the electro-industrial scope, oriented as goods move. Trade selections (a new or
expanded relationship in the optimized allocation) give 1{,}704 pairs, and investment selections (the
leading host serving an investor, turned round so that the host supplies the investor) 669; 344 pairs
carry both. Each economy's roles are kept apart rather than ranked (Figure~\ref{fig:aib_roles}): 105
economies supply through trade, the 57 economies of the scope buy and invest, and 32 host a leading
increment under one of the two simulated shocks. These counts include every positive role; the
dashboard names a role only where it reaches at least one percentage point of node demand (the share
of a node's demand the economy serves or receives under the headline shock, summed over nodes) and
lists smaller roles as minor. Twenty-five economies hold all four roles. Economies
outside the coalition provide 14.4 percent of the USD 159 billion of added trade, and the 44 that only
supply are all outside it.

Added trade concentrates among a few suppliers: Korea adds USD 19.1 billion to 43 partners and Japan
USD 16.7 billion to 55, followed by the United States (USD 11.2 billion), Germany (USD 10.5 billion),
Italy and France. India, Singapore and the United States are the largest buyers of that trade, and the
largest single pair is Korea to India (USD 12.0 billion over 20 sub-sectors). The hosts are a
different group. The economies that would host the most new capacity, Hungary, Czechia, the
Philippines and Viet Nam, with USD 26 to 29 billion each, are minor suppliers of added trade, while the
largest investors, the United States (USD 32.2 billion across 25 nodes), Germany and Mexico, are also
buyers. The roles answer different questions, which is why the network reports them separately and
gives no single ranking.

\subsection{Node classification}
\label{subsec:aib_classes}

Each node receives one of four policy treatments, by rules applied in order to the headline results:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textsc{low intervention} if the shock removes less than 15 percent of demand;
  \item \textsc{build} if the residual gap after optimized trade is 10 percent or more: new capacity
  is needed, with coordinated finance, guarantees and long-term offtake at the listed hosts;
  \item \textsc{manage specialization} if the node is resilient but its optimized supply is
  concentrated (the largest supplier holds 40 percent or more, the three largest 75 percent or more,
  or diversifying to a 40 percent limit costs five points or more): inventories, supply guarantees and
  reciprocal access with the dominant suppliers;
  \item \textsc{low intervention} if existing relationships already restore 90 percent;
  \item \textsc{coordinate} if the coordination dividend is five points or more: market access, rules
  of origin, procurement and offtake with the listed partners;
  \item otherwise \textsc{low intervention}: existing trade is nearly sufficient.
\end{enumerate}
Concentration is tested before adequate existing resilience because a node that copes through a few
suppliers is a specialization to manage, not one to leave alone. A node can need several instruments,
so the needs are also kept as labels (\textsc{coordinate} for a dividend of five points, \textsc{build}
for a gap of 10 percent, \textsc{manage specialization} for the concentration test, each when exposure
is at least 15 percent), and the continuous results stay beside the class.

Of the 92 nodes, 40 are \textsc{build}, 50 \textsc{low intervention}, one \textsc{manage
specialization} (oil storage and transport equipment) and one has insufficient data; none is
\textsc{coordinate}. In the electro-industrial scope 37 nodes are \textsc{build} and 32 \textsc{low
intervention}; the median \textsc{build} node loses 27.8 percent of its traded supply and keeps a gap of
23.4 percent. Lithium chemicals and rare-earth metals need \textsc{build} and \textsc{manage
specialization} together, and wind towers, \textsc{low intervention} because existing relationships
restore 93 percent, keep \textsc{coordinate} as a need. Polysilicon and cobalt and nickel refining are
\textsc{low intervention} on low trade exposure while China dominates their production, so both carry
the physical-capability warning: their dependence arrives embodied in wafers, cells and battery
materials. The classes are interpretations of the continuous results, made with stated thresholds;
Section~\ref{subsec:robust_network} shows how they move with the settings.

\subsection{Sectoral Compacts}
\label{subsec:aib_compacts}

A Sectoral Compact is warranted only where a group of nodes and countries gains from acting together.
The intervention nodes (\textsc{build}, \textsc{coordinate} and \textsc{manage specialization}) of the
strategic scope are grouped by complete linkage, so that every pair of nodes in a compact passes three
tests. \emph{Chain coherence}: one node is upstream of the other within three links of a curated,
directed physical and process chain (rare-earth oxide to metal and alloy to permanent magnet to motor
or generator; lithium minerals to chemicals to cathode to cell to pack to vehicle; graphite to anode to
cell; silicon metal to polysilicon to wafer to cell to module; copper and electrical steel to cables
and transformers), or both feed the same downstream node directly; sharing a technology or a stage is
not enough. \emph{Partner overlap}: a cosine similarity of at least 0.35 between the nodes' recurring
supplier vectors (optimized supply shares summed over every shock, with the leading hosts), or
between one node's suppliers and the other's buyers. \emph{Policy coherence}: a cosine similarity of at
least 0.5 between the nodes' policy-need profiles (dividend, residual gap, excess concentration). A
compact must also be one connected piece of the chain. A technology can therefore yield several
compacts or none.

\paragraph{Exact minimum coalitions.} A compact protects its \emph{demand anchors}, the importers
holding 60 percent of its lost supply (at most six). The anchors' coverage in a node when a set of
countries coordinates is linear in membership: what reaches them with nobody coordinating, plus what
each member adds by redirecting its available supply and, where it is a leading host of a node that
needs capacity, one 10 percent increment. The \emph{minimum coalition} is then the solution of a binary
program: the fewest countries such that every node of the compact reaches 95 percent of the anchors'
traded demand, with the anchors always members. Among coalitions of that size a second program picks
the most robust, maximizing the smallest margin over the target across the compact's nodes, so that the
coalition does not sit exactly on the target where losing any small supplier would tip a node below it;
remaining ties go to the coalition adding the most supply (Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm}). A greedy path,
adding the economy that most reduces the shortfall, is kept for the view of coverage as countries
join.

\paragraph{Tiers.} The exact 95 percent coalition is the \emph{core}. Within it, a \emph{critical supply
partner} is a member that is needed (without its supply the rest miss the target) and that no single
other candidate can replace; in a minimum coalition every member is needed by construction, so
substitutability is what distinguishes them. \emph{Associate partners} are the fewest additions that
reach 99 percent. A \emph{critical external dependency} is a non-member whose loss would push a node
below 95 percent with the coalition fixed and that supplies at least 1 percent of the anchors' demand.
A group is a candidate compact only if its coalition lifts the anchors' coverage by at least five
points over what their existing relationships give them, and a coalition of fewer than three countries
is a bilateral arrangement rather than a compact.

\paragraph{Results.} The 37 intervention nodes of the scope form 22 candidate compacts, nine of
them spanning several nodes (Figure~\ref{fig:aib_compacts} and Table~\ref{tab:aib_compacts}). Every one
is an Investment / Capacity compact, since every intervention node in the scope is \textsc{build}, and
all carry the same instruments: coordinated finance, guarantees, an investment pipeline, price support,
demand aggregation and long-term offtake. Coalitions are small: three to nine countries, six in 13 of the
22. The exact coalition reaches 95 percent in all 22 and the robust tie-break is solved in all 22; the
greedy path finds coalitions of the same size but with different members in nine. Twenty-nine economies
belong to at least one coalition, led by Viet Nam (16 compacts), the United States (15), Japan (14),
Germany (13), India and Korea (10 each). Critical supply partners appear in four compacts: Singapore and
Taiwan in the battery compact (cells, foils, electrolyte, separators and packs), the United Kingdom in
motors and magnets, Madagascar and Mozambique in anodes with natural graphite, and Viet Nam in rare-earth
metals. Seven compacts have a critical external dependency, most of all motors and magnets, where seven
non-members (Switzerland, France, Italy, Malaysia, the Philippines, Poland and Thailand) are critical;
Thailand is also the one associate partner of any compact. Rare-earth compounds and metals sit apart
from magnets because their partners and needs differ from their chain neighbours'.

\paragraph{The coordination dividend of a compact.} Coordination within a coalition lifts the
anchors' coverage by a median of 32.6 points (10.5 to 76.4) over their fragmented baseline, but that
gain is almost entirely members redirecting their own exports toward each other. For all importers of
the compact's nodes, the trade compact adds at most 0.04 points to what existing relationships
achieve, in every compact: trade coordination moves supply among allies and away from others, and adds
none to the system. What adds supply is the investment response, a median of 19.9 points of the
nodes' demand, which brings 12 of the 22 compacts to 95 percent or more. The compacts are therefore
best read as investment and offtake clubs for specific chains, with the coalition naming the members
whose supply or capacity is indispensable and the external dependencies naming the risks that
membership does not remove.

\subsection{What the analysis does and does not show}
\label{subsec:aib_limitations}

The analysis measures what the 2024 trade network can do after a stated shock; it does not measure
what production would cost, how long capacity takes to build or what governments are willing to pay.
Demand is traded demand, so domestic production used at home is outside it. HS6 headings mix grades
and uses: rare-earth compounds mix concentrates and separated oxides, and cables and converters serve
many technologies; the data-quality flags say where. Shocks are applied node by node with no coupling
across nodes, so a magnet shortage does not by itself reduce motor exports. Export Opportunity and
Friendshore are technology-stage indices, not sub-sector ones. The physical rule decides who can
supply, not how much: a listed producer keeps its full trade response even where its production is
small, and the 81 nodes without credible physical data, among them magnets, motors and most
equipment, keep their trade-only result. The investment increment is synthetic and the same size
everywhere, so the investment step ranks hosts and shows the order of magnitude of what capacity adds;
