\section{The Allied Industrial Base: further results}\label{oa:network}

\paragraph{Nodes and data quality.} Of the 92 nodes, 23 are of high confidence, 68 usable with
caution and one insufficient for optimization (cobalt ores and concentrates). The caution flags are
mirror flows above 30 percent of value, comparative advantage missing for exporters with 20 percent
or more of exports, codes kept with a caveat or shared with another node (wafers, HS 3818.00, also
counted in Semiconductors), HS revisions without a 2020 equivalent, and production far more
concentrated than trade. Five cells are shared physical nodes: permanent magnets, rare-earth compounds
and rare-earth metals (Electric Vehicles and Wind), transformers (Electric Grid and Solar) and pipelines
(Oil and Gas). Eleven nodes have credible physical data: anode materials, battery cells, cobalt and
nickel refining, lithium chemicals, natural graphite, rare-earth metals, rare-earth compounds (with the
monazite ores merged into them), polysilicon, solar cells, solar modules and wafers.

\paragraph{Validation chains.} Four nodes are traced through every step under both supply constraints
(Table~\ref{tab:aib_validation}); they report what the model produced, and nothing was tuned to them.
Permanent magnets have no physical proxy, so the two modes agree: China's removal costs 52.4 percent of
the allied scope's traded demand, existing relationships and optimized trade both restore half of it,
the coordination dividend is zero and the node is \textsc{build}, in the motors-and-magnets compact
whose critical supply partner is the United Kingdom. Rare-earth compounds are \textsc{build} in both
modes: under hold the concentrates that Myanmar and Laos sell to China stay with China, and in the
production-constrained mode the exporters absent from the IEA refining data cannot expand, so the gap,
38.2 percent, is separation capacity; Japan, Malaysia and the United States form the minimum coalition.
Anode materials lose 59.4 percent of their supply and keep a gap of 58.3 percent; with natural graphite
they form a compact in which Madagascar and Mozambique are critical supply partners. Wafers show what the
physical rule changes. On trade alone, semiconductor wafers from Japan, Taiwan and Korea appear to
replace Chinese solar wafers, coverage reaches 93.4 percent and the node is \textsc{low intervention}
with the physical-capability warning; with solar-wafer capacity deciding who can supply, coverage stops at
74.5 percent and the node is \textsc{build}, with silicon metal and solar cells in a compact of India,
Japan, Korea, Thailand, Turkey and Taiwan.

\paragraph{The investment step.} In the headline, 53 nodes have a residual gap of at least 5 percent,
49 of them in the electro-industrial scope, with up to 20 eligible hosts each (a median of 20, at least
11). The leading hosts qualify on comparative advantage at material scale in 41 nodes, on production
shares in five, on both in one and on world export share in two. A 10 percent increment at the leading
host lowers the median residual gap from 18.7 to 8.7 percent and closes it in 10 nodes; the smallest gain
of a leading host is 5.9 points, where the gap was smaller than the increment. The increments of 5 and 20
percent are in \texttt{allied\_investment\_partnerships.csv}.

\paragraph{Coalitions.} The exact and greedy coalitions have the same size in all 22 compacts and the
same members in 13. Associate partners, the additions that reach 99 percent,
are needed in one compact (Thailand, in motors and magnets); in the other 21 the 95 percent coalition
already reaches 99 percent. Critical external dependencies are reported in seven compacts; the full
leave-one-country-out test, for every supplier of every node, is in
\texttt{aib\_compact\_leave\_one\_out.csv}.
