\section{Discussion, next steps and conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

This section reads the results through the analytical design for an Allied Industrial Base of
the New Energy Industrial Strategy working paper \citep{neis2026allied}, states what the paper does
not establish, and sets out the extensions that design calls for.

\subsection{What the results imply for an allied industrial base}\label{subsec:discussion_implications}

\paragraph{Managed interdependence rather than autarky.} The working paper defines the objective
as managed interdependence at allied scale: strategically consequential capabilities should exist
at sufficient scale somewhere in a trusted network, not in every member, because no single ally
can reproduce China's scale across the electro-industrial stack
\citep{neis2026allied,campbell2025underestimating}. China leads 22 of the 33 Economic Opportunity pairs and 11
of the 30 Energy Security pairs (Sections~\ref{sec:es} and~\ref{sec:eo}); a coalition member leads
16 security pairs but only 8 opportunity pairs. Within each member, opportunity and security need
move in opposite directions: the median within-country Spearman correlation between $\EO$ and
$1-\ES$ is $-0.49$ and negative for every member (Section~\ref{sec:strategic}). A member's strongest
opportunities lie where it is already secure, and its needs lie where others are strong. The
network design makes this arithmetic explicit (Section~\ref{sec:network}). It selects seven to ten
producers per stage among 37 nodes, with a median designed Herfindahl index of 0.152 (6.6
equivalent producers), close by calibration to the nodes' own export concentration. Self-supply is
positive in two stages only, a consequence of scoring a node's self-supply at the median edge weight
and of the binding dual-sourcing rule (793 of the 811 consumer--stage pairs draw on exactly two
partners) rather than an empirical finding; under these rules every node draws most of its
requirement from other members.

The closed network is not the world market, however. In the median basket stage the 37 nodes
account for 74 percent of world exports (54 to 97 percent), and China is the largest world exporter
in 10 of the 18 basket stages, including eight of the nine Midstream ones, with shares of 20 to 43
percent. The design therefore replaces between 3 and 46 percent of observed world supply by
assumption; whether the designated nodes could supply it, at what cost and how fast, lies outside
the model. The evidence that broad relocalisation is costly and that diversification usually serves
resilience better than reshoring \citep{oecd2025supply,thakurweigold2024promoting,aiyar2023geoeconomic}
applies to an allied wall as much as to a national one.

\paragraph{The node, not the sector, as the unit of treatment.} The working paper argues that
policy should be set by node, because one technology contains very different risks
\citep{neis2026allied}. Our stages are coarser than its nodes, but they show the same heterogeneity.
Technology and stage explain 44 percent of the variance of $\ES$ and 58 percent of $\EO$, against 12
and 18 percent for countries alone, and concentration sits in different places along each chain.
Midstream security is mostly a trade question (the Trade category supplies 73 percent of the
Midstream score), and China dominates the Midstream baskets. Upstream concentration lies with
resource holders inside the network: Canada supplies 54 percent of the nodes' Nuclear Upstream
exports, Australia 41 percent of Coal Upstream, and the EU 48 percent of Nuclear Midstream.
Chinese-parent ownership of foreign-invested plants is high in solar (median share 0.75 across
suppliers) and nil in wind (Section~\ref{sec:bilateral}).

Node-level treatment departs from naive rankings in two places. First, the need reading of the
Strategic Index rates a stage highest where a member lacks the resource. Without the endowment
screen 47 of the 57 allied picks would be Upstream, 17 of them in Nuclear Upstream, many in members
with no uranium. With the screen 43 picks are Midstream, and 41 of the 57 picks change (Figure~\ref{fig:strategic_screen}). The
eight Nuclear Upstream picks that remain all hold identified uranium resources, but small ones
(0.015 to 0.79 percent of the world's) and none with recorded mine production in 2019--2024, so
they are still need-driven: the screen removes the no-resource artefact but not its small-resource
counterpart. The screen is a rule of pick eligibility and leaves every index value unchanged. Nor
are the picks an allied assignment: 27 members would prioritise Batteries Midstream, which the
network design gives to nine producer nodes, and only 28 of the 57 members hold a designed share in
their pick stage. Second, the network
does not reproduce the export ranking: the median Spearman correlation between designed production
shares and observed export shares is 0.53, and in the median basket stage 66 percent of designed
production goes to nodes outside the three largest exporting nodes. The design applies no endowment
screen, so 21 of its 90 roles in the screened Upstream stages are held by nodes without a recorded
resource base; their shares would have to be sourced through trade or overseas equity.

\paragraph{Partners in the designed network.} The working paper asks
an allied design to ``optimize combinations, not rankings'', valuing a partner for the capability
it adds rather than for its stand-alone score \citep{neis2026allied}. As bilateral partners the 26
partner-tier members rank low: their median rank in the headline Partnership Strength Index is 64.5
of 192, against 16 for the core members, in part because UNGA alignment makes up on average 24
percent of the Friendshore composite. Yet, in a design that scores capability rather than current
capacity, the partner tier carries 58 percent of designed Upstream
production and 45 percent of Midstream production, and nodes outside the high-income group carry 47
and 43 percent. Eight nodes produce only Upstream, all resource economies (Australia, Chile, Saudi
Arabia, Peru, Argentina, Oman, Namibia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo); eight others
produce nothing and belong to the network as consumers. One caveat qualifies the reading: the
producer score carries no alignment term, so Indonesia, which enters through IPEF and is
China-leaning by its votes, leads six Upstream stages.

\paragraph{The three hypotheses.} The working paper proposes three hypotheses for testing first
\citep{neis2026allied}. The paper tests none of them; its results bear on each to a different
degree. On the first, that allied specialisation reaches a resilience target at lower public cost
than parallel national programmes, the design shows that seven to ten producers per stage reproduce
the nodes' observed concentration and leave no consumer on a single supplier, but it contains no
costs, capacities or outlays; the 1.9 percent of the objective that the portfolio caps forgo is
score, not money. On the second, that coordinating demand matters more than coordinating subsidies,
the results are silent: the Policy index folds every policy type into one strength score, and
network demand is an index of need and size, not a volume. On the third, that rules targeted at
high-severity nodes deliver most of the benefit of broad decoupling at lower cost, the results
document a precondition, heterogeneity across stages (the Strategic Index is 73 percent sector and 4
percent country variance), but not the benefit or cost of either rule, because the design excludes
China from every stage, itself a sector-wide rule.

\paragraph{Normative choices and how readers can vary them.} The weights of the Strategic Index,
its need reading, the alignment weight in Friendshore, the diversification weight $\lambda$, the
portfolio caps and the coalition thresholds are choices. As the working paper argues, such weights
are political, and analysis should display the frontier of choices rather than one number
\citep{neis2026allied}. Some choices matter a great deal (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}): reading
the security term as strength rather than need keeps only 5 of the 57 picks; random draws of the
five Strategic Index weights keep a member's pick in 73 percent of draws on average; dropping
alignment from Friendshore moves China from 44th to 3rd in the Partnership Strength Index. The
interactive explorer that accompanies the paper exposes these choices through four dials in its
Strategic Insights module: \emph{hawkishness} weights security (the need term, Friendshore,
alignment and Chinese-parent exposure, the diversification weight), \emph{bullishness} opportunity
(the $\EO$ term, partnership opportunity, market size in network demand), \emph{populism} policy
signals and home manufacturing, and \emph{greenness} climate performance, dropping coal, oil and gas
at its highest settings. The paper's headline is one setting of these dials. The descriptive Sector
Insights module shows the theme data (trade flows, reserves, production, investment including the
CM-FDI projects, policy records) by technology and stage without index weights, so that a reader
can inspect the evidence behind any score.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{subsec:discussion_limitations}

\emph{No outcome validation.} The indices are not validated against disruptions, investment or
export growth. The one predictive test, in the study of vertical complementarities
(Section~\ref{subsec:robust_vertical}), is a warning: sourcing-weighted input security did not
predict the import shortfalls of 2021--22, whereas sourcing concentration did.

\emph{Data vintages and source conventions.} Inputs come from different years (92 percent of
opportunity cells mix years; fossil reserves date from end-2020, mineral reserves from end-2025),
and the 2020 and 2025 vintages agree less for $\EO$ (median within-pair Spearman 0.87) than for
$\ES$ (0.99). Holdings the USGS does not quantify (not available, withheld or small) count as zero
in the technology roll-ups. The IEA's 2035 announced manufacturing-capacity shares are regional for
Europe, so every EU member carries the EU's share (for example 0.15 in Wind Midstream). The
BloombergNEF outlook behind Downstream Consumption does not report Canada, whose category is imputed
in all eight cells it enters, leaving six of Canada's nine scored Downstream security cells
unrankable.

\emph{Mirror trade.} Economies that do not report to UN Comtrade enter the trade matrix only
through their partners' declarations: 65 of 208 importers in 2024, among them two coalition members
(Gabon and Viet Nam). Electricity is excluded from the grid basket, and Hong Kong's re-exports from
the export measures.

\emph{A knife-edge $\lambda$.} The calibrated value 0.78 meets the target by a small margin (0.77
misses it by 0.0002), the median designed concentration is not monotone in $\lambda$, and the pick
moved with every input vintage. The design itself is stable: at 0.77, 21 of the 22 producer sets
are unchanged.

\emph{Policy basis.} The Policy index of 150 economies, 11 of them coalition members, rests on IEA
PAMS alone. For the 65 economies with both components, ranking their cells on IEA PAMS alone rather
than on the composite gives a median within-country Spearman correlation of only 0.48.

\emph{Thin and construction-driven picks.} The pick is within 0.005 of its runner-up for 10 of the
57 members and within 0.01 for 21. The Nuclear Midstream picks of Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic,
Guatemala and Namibia are not an Energy Security floor effect. Each may pick only four sectors, the
Midstream stages of nuclear, oil, gas and coal (Namibia also Nuclear Upstream); Midstream nuclear $\ES$ is compressed around its
median; and the technology weight (0.999 for nuclear against 0.42 for gas, 0.02 for coal and 0 for
oil) decides the pick. The picks reflect a narrow choice set, not an opportunity in nuclear fuel.

\emph{Rank-compressed EU demand.} The EU node holds 24.6 percent of the nodes' GDP but a median
demand weight of 0.035, about that of the United States: network demand is a ranking, not a
volume.

\emph{Coalition boundary.} Taiwan (no UN vote, no listed agreement, at most 1.5 percent of any
world stage) and Switzerland (whose only supplier route rested on electricity exports) are outside
the coalition.

\emph{Layers not yet measured.} Of the working paper's five analytical layers, the paper measures
node severity only as country-relative exposure (concentration and dependence, ranked within each
node), capability through $\EO$, and partner
complementarity through the bilateral indices and the network. It does not measure the fourth and
fifth, bankability and robustness under stress (recovery time), nor common-mode risk beyond the
ownership of foreign-invested plants or the dynamic option value of latent capacity.

\subsection{Next steps and recommendations}\label{subsec:discussion_next}

The working paper orders the decision in three steps, severity, coordination and specialisation
\citep{neis2026allied}. This paper offers a first, score-based version of the last step and partial measures for the
first two.
Table~\ref{tab:discussion_agenda} lists ten extensions drawn from the working paper's
implementation questions, analytical upgrades and research agenda, with the data and model change
each requires. Five deserve emphasis.

First, a \emph{complementarity matrix}. The network can already price a node's marginal
contribution: re-solving the design without each node, or with each candidate added, measures what
it adds to the network objective. A full solve of the 22 stages takes about 13 minutes at the
headline setting, so the matrix is affordable, and it would replace the stand-alone ranking of
partners with the gap-filling value the working paper asks for.

Second, \emph{common-mode risk and resilience floors}. Two suppliers in two countries do not
diversify supply if they share a parent, a precursor or an equipment maker
\citep{neis2026allied,farrell2019weaponized}. The Chinese-parent component of Friendshore covers
the first channel in four Midstream stages; the network should also cap, by constraint, the
designed share resting on any one parent headquarters or precursor source. The redundancy rule of
the design (at least two suppliers per consumer) is a first resilience floor. Floors tied to
recovery time need volumes, capacities and inventories in place of unit demand, so that removing
the largest producer for 6, 12 or 24 months yields unmet demand rather than a change in score. The
two other upgrades need new inputs. \emph{Dynamic option value}, the time and cost of ramping or
requalifying capacity, calls for a multi-period design with build lead times, which the CM-FDI
announcement and completion years could calibrate. \emph{Bankability} needs the cost of capital,
permitting times, offtake and delivery capacity by country and stage \citep{spokas2025systemic},
entered as a node-score component or a feasibility constraint.

Third, an \emph{AIB Network Optimization Pilot} for the three chains the working paper proposes:
rare earths and magnets, batteries and anodes, and solar wafers. Our baskets already isolate the
magnet rare earths (Wind and Electric Vehicles Upstream), permanent magnets (Wind and Electric
Vehicles Midstream), anode materials (Batteries Midstream) and polysilicon and wafers (Solar
Midstream), but each chain must be split into sub-stages (separation, alloy, magnet; graphite,
spherical graphite, anode; polysilicon, ingot and wafer, cell) with plant-level capacity and
qualification times. The output would be a portfolio per chain: capacity by node, locations,
minimum redundancy, priority projects and financing gaps.

Fourth, \emph{differentiated Gates and Fences and sectoral compacts}. Combined with severity data
on consequence and substitutability, the stage-level evidence assembled here (concentration, China's
export share, Chinese-parent exposure, alignment) can sort stages into high, medium and low fences.
The designed flows identify the partner sets for compacts with pooled demand and finance, and the
build candidates (led by India, Indonesia and Viet Nam) show where development finance would back a
designated role.

Fifth, a \emph{system scorecard with off-ramps}. A recurring rerun already produces several of the
working paper's system metrics: supplier counts, designed and observed concentration, the largest
share and the trusted share of world supply. With inventory coverage, recovery time and the price
premium added, it could trigger reviews automatically, for instance when the nodes' observed
concentration in a stage rises above the calibration target, and retire support when it falls.

The hypotheses can then be tested. Forcing each node to supply its own demand gives the
self-sufficiency counterfactual for the first, once costs enter. CM-FDI project histories can be
set against demand instruments and supply subsidies for the second. Admitting China and other non-members as nodes, fenced stage by
stage rather than excluded, would test the third, and varying the redundancy rule would show when
another allied supplier stops reducing risk. The study of vertical complementarities adds its own
agenda (last row of the table).

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{subsec:conclusion}

This paper has built indices of energy security and economic opportunity for 219 economies, eleven
technologies and three supply-chain stages; derived a coalition of 57 members from UN voting and
supply shares; rated every ordered pair of economies as suppliers and markets; and designed an
allied division of labour across 22 Upstream and Midstream stages. The results support the working
paper's premise in a limited sense: no member is both secure and competitive across the chains, and
a design that penalises concentration assigns roles very differently from a ranking of current
exporters. Whether members' capabilities are complementary in the working paper's sense, and whether
such a design is cheaper, more bankable or faster to recover, needs the complementarity matrix,
costs, volumes and time. The
indices and the network are best read as the analytical backbone of a
recurring review, with the explorer showing how its answers change when the weights change.
