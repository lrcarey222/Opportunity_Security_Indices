<h2 id="framework">From evidence to a strategic choice</h2>

The strategy starts with evidence, not policy. Need asks whether dependence or deployment creates a real problem. Prize asks whether productive capability has strategic industrial value. Position asks how strong the country already is. Those three determine the choice. Only then do we compare the recommendation with what government is actually doing.

No country needs to make everything, so "rely on trusted partners" and "keep it open" are answers in their own right, not failures to build at home. The same framework runs for every country, each of 11 technologies and each of six supply-chain stages (Extraction, Refining & Processing, Component Manufacturing, Final Assembly & Equipment, Deployment & Use, Recovery & Recycling), at fixed thresholds and with no overall score. One technology at one stage is a supply-chain step (a *node* in the data).

1. **Need: what problem has to be solved?** At the five producing stages, Need means supply-chain vulnerability; at Deployment & Use, how urgently the domestic energy system needs more deployment. Two separate measures, never combined into one score. *Supply-chain need* reads import dependence, supplier concentration, exposure to China and opposite-bloc suppliers and the lack of trusted alternatives, each a percentile among the countries at the step; a high percentile counts only where the need score itself reaches 0.10, so a tiny exposure in a thinly traded step does not read as high need. *Deployment need* reads demand adequacy (current policy), the gap to national targets (national commitments: Ember's 2030 targets) and system requirements. Each family names its scenario and its basis (national data, a regional proxy, an observed trend or global context); a climate-aligned value is shown only as a labelled alternative, and a missing family is not assessed, never zero.
2. **Prize: is productive capability here strategically valuable?** Market growth matters, but so do process learning, supplier ecosystems and future leadership. The prize is the *Strategic Industrial Value*, from three families. The market prize, *Industrial Opportunity*: the step's expected world demand growth to 2035 under current policy (IEA World Energy Outlook 2025, Stated Policies; the IEA critical-minerals outlook for mineral extraction and refining), with investment momentum where it is observed, lifted by national home-market growth where national data exist; a declining market is capped below the bar. Industrial commons: process knowledge, the workforce and supplier ecosystem and cross-sector spillover. Future option value: whether a credible leap ahead is in reach. The value is a percentile across the producing steps; where neither commons nor option reaches the gate it is capped just below the prize bar, so market size alone never makes a prize high. It reads no current capability and no need variable; technology maturity is context, not a score. It is not the Economic Opportunity score described below. A *large-yard case* weighs against it: a mature, passive capability where most of eight commodity signals hold (standardized, substitutable, quick to qualify, diversified world supply, little process learning, little cross-sector spillover, little product differentiation, commoditized) is Watch whatever the size of its market, unless the country already holds a top-quarter position; it is kept with a capability floor where industrial learning is moderate, and a credible leap or strong learning vetoes it.
3. **Position: how much real capability does the country have today?** *Competitive Position*, demonstrated capability only: export share and specialization, production share, operating manufacturing capacity, investment, cost, resources and deployment scale where the stage calls for them, placed among the step's contenders (the economies with at least 0.5% of world supply). Its trade evidence is a 2022-24 structural benchmark of exports, world share and revealed comparative advantage, with current trade momentum taking 15% of the trade family where a matched 12-month comparison exists.
4. **Choice: what should the country do?** Six choices, each an action. *Secure* comes first and overrides the rest. It needs an acute consequence and one of three routes: control-system risk (a foreign actor could command or disable the step, or it serves national-security end uses), chokepoint risk (a potentially coercive supplier could withhold it, substitution is hard and trusted supply too slow to restore: concentration alone is not enough) or system resilience (an essential function, heavy import dependence, years to rebuild and too little trusted supply). Secure does not mean reshoring: it is delivered at home, with allies, from a diversified market or a mix. At Deployment & Use, high deployment need is **Accelerate** (deployment itself is the priority; it says nothing about making the equipment at home), otherwise **Watch**; a deployment step is never Partner. At the producing stages need and prize triage the step and Competitive Position decides the response. With a high prize, a position in the top quarter of the contenders (and above the capability floor) is **Preserve**; below it, a credible pathway is **Catch Up**: a capability anchor (at least 0.5% of world supply or exports, 0.5% of world operating capacity, 1% in committed capacity, an adjacent stage with 5% of world supply or, at Extraction, a recorded resource base, which Extraction always needs) plus a corroborating signal of another kind (product-space feasibility, an input-cost advantage, learning inputs, a relatively strong adjacent stage, announced capacity), so Catch Up always has something real to build on; otherwise high need is **Partner** and low need **Watch**. With a low prize, high need is Partner (a strong position is kept as Preserve and flagged for consistency review) and low need is Watch. Watch is a positive conclusion: low domestic priority, with access kept affordable and diversified. A step whose need or prize is not measured is *not assessed*, never Watch. In the data the six choices sit in three families: Secure, Compete (Preserve, Catch Up, Partner) and Deploy (Accelerate, Watch).
5. **Policy alignment: is policy focused on the right places?** Is government action matched to the problem, or using the wrong amount or type of intervention? Observed policy is compared afterwards and never enters the choice. Secure, Preserve or Catch Up with weak policy *needs more attention*, as does Accelerate with weak deployment policy (deployment policy lags); Partner met with heavy localization is the *wrong tool for the problem*; Watch under heavy protection or localization is *too much intervention*. Thin policy records give *not enough policy evidence* or a low confidence, never a guess.

### Three yards: different problems, different fences

A strong industrial strategy is as much about deciding what not to build as deciding what to build. The choices fall into three yards (the Yards & Fences framing): a Small yard behind a High fence (Secure); a Medium yard behind a medium, permeable fence (Preserve, Catch Up, Partner, and Accelerate at Deployment & Use); a Large yard behind a Low fence (Watch). The yards are different policy problems, not low, medium and high scores on one scale.

* **Small yard, High fence.** Capabilities where foreign control or concentrated dependence could create an unacceptable security or resilience risk.
* **Medium yard, medium and permeable fence.** Capabilities worth building or preserving because they shape competitiveness, industrial learning or future technological leadership.
* **Large yard, Low fence.** Capabilities where affordable and diversified access generally matters more than domestic production.

A technology can sit in more than one yard. Capability layers, such as grid-control software or battery-management firmware, are yard units of their own with their own choice, so a step whose layers sit in different yards is mixed: the Strategy Map splits its cell diagonally, and the fence sections list such a layer behind its own fence. A control layer is in the Small yard only where the data show coercive foreign control for that country. Each choice carries its policy design (a fence, a gate and a build, the fence height following the yard) and its delivery.

### Where allies fit

Partner steps name up to three best-placed trusted partners from the Allied Industrial Base (or world supply where the base does not cover the step), ranked on capability, relationship strength and how much they would diversify the country's supply; a step with no credible allied solution is a *partner gap*, reported rather than forced into a solution. The allied division of labor counts, per step, the countries whose choice is Partner (and Secure): the partner demand that allied supply has to meet. Accelerate is a deployment priority and is not counted. How allies divide the work, and where coordinated trade or new capacity closes the remaining gaps, is set out under Allied Industrial Base, Sectoral Compacts, About.

<h2 id="legacy-osi">Security and opportunity scores</h2>

Beside the strategic choices, the Explorer shows a descriptive view built from indices: the Energy Security and Economic Opportunity scores, the Strategic Index built on them, the partner scores and the four corners of the Security and opportunity chart. These scores describe relative security and opportunity; the strategic choices above are the recommendation. The corners map loosely onto the choices: Grow ≈ Preserve, Upgrade ≈ Catch Up, Exposed ≈ Partner, Stable ≈ Watch. The results differ because each index mixes capability, vulnerability, production, trade, investment, demand, reserves, concentration and deployment in one score, which the framework above separates into need, prize and position. On a Country profile the scores sit under Evidence & Methodology, with the dials as scenarios to explore; the pages that show them for every economy (Overview, Country scores, Security vs opportunity, Strategic Index and Partners) are labelled "Security and opportunity scores". The rest of this page describes those indices (Energy Security, Economic Opportunity, the Strategic Index and the partnership measures) and their four dials. The dials never change the strategic choices.

## The indices in plain words

Every economy gets two scores in each of 66 sectors (11 energy technologies, each at the six stages of its supply chain: extraction, refining, components, assembly, deployment and recovery): **Energy Security (ES)**, how well it can rely on what it needs there, and **Economic Opportunity (EO)**, how well placed it is to create value there; both run from 0 to 1 and compare it with every other economy in the same sector. A few sectors have no value for a score (most geothermal stages, for example); they are shown as not scored, never dropped. The **Strategic Index (SI)** then ranks each economy's own sectors by how strongly the data point to prioritising them: high opportunity, high exposure (security need, $1-ES$), existing policy activity, the stage (manufacturing, at the components and assembly stages, weighs most) and the technology's low-emission value. The **Partnership Strength Index (PSI)** rates how good a partner one economy is for another, as a friendshoring opportunity, a partner where it could invest and source to meet its security need (**Friendshore, FS**), as a market for its exports (**Partnership Opportunity, PO**) and, for middle- and lower-income partners, as a development partner (**Development Potential, DP**). Four **dials** (hawkishness, bullishness, populism and climate, also called greenness), set from the Perspective control of a Country profile, re-weight all of this to reflect different priorities. They never change the observed or modelled facts: trade, production, concentration, the ES and EO values, and the Allied Industrial Base model; nor the strategic posture. At 50 every dial reproduces the pipeline's six-stage baseline exactly (the baseline); any other setting is a custom perspective recomputed in your browser.

## What the dials do

The indices are built from a chain of choices: how much weight to put on security against opportunity, on industrial policy, on climate. The baseline makes one set of those choices. This explorer lets you make your own, and recomputes everything from the same data with the same method.

The Allied Industrial Base section shows how the coalition absorbs a supply shock with the trade it has, where new investment would close what is left, which country plays which role, and the Sectoral Compacts. Those results are modelled facts; the dials do not move them.

Four dials express a reader's priorities. Each runs from 0 to 100, and **50 is the baseline**: the pipeline's default weights at the six stages. With every dial at 50 and the advanced settings at their defaults, every index value is the pipeline's output, exported exactly. Any other setting is a custom perspective, recomputed in your browser; the pages that follow it can show the perspective, the baseline, or the change between them (the **Show** control at the top of the page). The perspective lives in the page address, so a copied link reproduces it.

## What the baseline rests on

**The coalition.** {{n_coalition}} economies: {{n_core}} core members (the U.S.-leaning bloc of the UN-vote ideal points, with GDP of at least \$100 bn) and {{n_partners}} partners. A partner is a member of a U.S. free-trade agreement, IPEF, the Minerals Security Partnership, the G7 or NATO with GDP of at least $100 bn, or a major supplier that does not lean towards China: at least 5% of world production, refining, manufacturing capacity or exports at a stage that makes or supplies something (every stage but deployment), or at least 10% of world reserves of a headline mineral or fuel. The reserves route adds Brazil (rare earths and graphite). The allied selection is national: every coalition member, EU members included, gets its own pick. The PSI's coalition view averages over the core members.

**Policy activity (the Policy index).** IEA Policies and Measures (PAMS, weight 3) and the Climate Action Tracker (CAT, weight 2), renormalised over the components present in each cell. The Tracker part depends on the technology: a strong climate rating scores high for low-emission technologies and low for coal, oil and gas. It measures how much policy a government has in a sector, not whether the policies work. IEA PAMS is required; CAT is added where it rates the country. Each scored economy's index therefore rests on **IEA PAMS + CAT** ({{n_policy_both}} economies in this build) or on **IEA PAMS only** ({{n_policy_pams_only}}); the Strategic Index and Country pages show which (the *policy basis*). The Strategic Index ranks policy within an economy, so the basis does not move one economy against another, but the two bases are different readings: compare policy levels across economies only within one basis. NIPO (Global Trade Alert) and the dual-use score are not components.

**The Strategic Index and the endowment screen.** The index keeps the need reading, $1-ES$: it favours the sectors where an economy is least secure. In an extraction sector an economy without the resource sits at the energy-security floor, so its need is high in exactly the sectors it cannot supply. The allied selection (each coalition member's top sector at any stage but deployment, Geothermal and Green Hydrogen aside) therefore applies an **endowment screen**: an extraction sector qualifies only where the economy has recorded reserves or production of that sector's resources (reserves from EI for oil, gas and coal, from the USGS *Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026* for critical minerals and from the Red Book for uranium; production from the critical-minerals production theme). The index values do not change. A screened-out cell keeps its rank in the economy's own list and is flagged *no resource base*. In this build {{n_screened_out}} of the {{n_screened}} scored extraction cells have no recorded resource base, and {{n_picked}} members have a pick ({{n_picked_extraction}} of them at extraction). The advanced panel can turn the screen off, a robustness variant.

**Data choices in this build.**

* Critical-mineral reserves come from the USGS *Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026*; the coalition's reserves route reads the same source.
* Every traded product code, theme and index variable sits at one of the six stages, the stage of what the product physically is: refined battery chemicals, polysilicon and electrical steel are refining, for example. Where a source reports one figure for manufacturing as a whole, components and assembly carry the same value.
* Electric Grid's material baskets are copper and aluminium: ores and concentrates at extraction, copper smelting and refining and primary aluminium at refining. Electricity (HS 271600), once its only material code, is excluded.
* Electric Grid's manufacturing baskets (components and assembly) exclude HS 851762 (telecom switching and routing equipment).
* Hong Kong is left out of the exporter-reported Comtrade export measures (export size, RCA, export concentration) as a re-export hub. Bilateral (dyad) trade in the partnership indices is unchanged.
* The trade-concentration (HHI) index is ranked across the cells of the 11 headline technologies only.
* Friendshore reads cross-border manufacturing investment from CM-FDI (P. Bandara and the Net Zero Industrial Policy Lab, Johns Hopkins University, edition 2026.10.05, CC BY 4.0) at the components and assembly stages of Solar, Wind, Batteries and Electric Vehicles: the 905 manufacturing projects announced, under construction, completed or operational (closed, paused and cancelled ones dropped; all announcement years; a joint venture counts half to its listed parent). The projects make both components and finished equipment, so both stages carry the same projects, counted once. They give the investment tie and the Chinese-parent ownership exposure described below; every other cell keeps the IMF direct-investment position. Economic Opportunity does not read CM-FDI.
* Material baskets cover Electric Vehicles (battery-mineral ores at extraction, magnet rare earths at extraction and refining), Wind (rare earths), Nuclear (uranium ores at extraction, natural uranium compounds at refining), Solar (silicon metal at refining) and Semiconductors (gallium, germanium and indium at refining; descriptive only, not in the indices): 259 six-digit basket codes in all. Some codes count in more than one sector, so baskets are never added up across sectors.
* Fossil-fuel production that the Energy Institute's *Statistical Review* does not report is taken from EIA International Energy Statistics, flagged as such and paired by year.
* The final runs (10-12 October 2026) corrected the theme tables found faulty while Sector Insights was built: mineral production as a demand-weighted world share, future-demand keys that collided between sources, IEA 2035 manufacturing shares over the world total including "Other", the electric-vehicle manufacturing placeholder region, the import-share variables (now self-sufficiency and the net-export ratio) and a tie in the energy-imports term, New Zealand's lending rate, and IMF price units taken from the indicator metadata. Every index here is built from the corrected tables.

## How a dial moves a weight

Every weight a dial owns is multiplied by

$$m(d) = 4^{(d-50)/50},$$

so $m(0)=0.25$, $m(25)=0.5$, $m(50)=1$, $m(75)=2$ and $m(100)=4$. Moving a dial 25 points halves or doubles every weight it owns, symmetrically. At 50 the multiplier is exactly one, so the baseline is reproduced bit for bit.

Only the five Strategic Index weights are renormalised explicitly, so that they keep summing to one:

$$w_k = \frac{w^0_k\, m_k}{\sum_j w^0_j\, m_j}\sum_j w^0_j .$$

Every other weight set (the Policy composite, the Friendshore and Opportunity components, the PSI) is already a weighted mean that divides by the sum of its weights, so scaling one weight changes its share and nothing else.

Hawkishness and bullishness move the two ends of the same ratio, Friendshore against Opportunity in the PSI. With both dials at the same setting that ratio is the baseline's.

## Hawkishness: security first

A hawk worries about supply security and coercion. Raising the dial puts more weight on where a country is insecure and on aligned partners. Inside Friendshore it scales the two security components alike: UN-vote alignment and the Chinese-parent ownership component (the share of a partner's foreign-owned clean-tech factories whose parent is headquartered in China, Hong Kong or Macao, scored as one minus the share). The ownership component's on/off switch is a robustness setting of the pipeline, not a dial.

A green reader rewards low-emission technologies and climate-policy ambition, and penalises partnerships in emission-intensive technologies.

## Presets

The preset menu sets the four dials at once: **Hawk** (hawkishness 90), **Bull** (bullishness 90), **Populist** (populism 90), **Green** (greenness 90, which also drops coal, oil and gas) and **Green hawk** (hawkishness and greenness 80). **Reset to baseline** returns every dial and advanced setting to the baseline.

## The formulas the explorer recomputes

**Pillars.** For pillar $P \in \{ES, EO\}$ and cell $(i,t,s)$, with category scores $c_{itsk}$ and weights $w^P_k$ over the categories observed for the cell,

$$P_{its} = \frac{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{C}^P_{its}} w^P_k\, c_{itsk}}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{C}^P_{its}} w^P_k}.$$

The category weights are in the advanced panel; the explorer recomputes the pillars exactly from the stored category scores. A cell is rankable if the economy is sovereign and the imputed share of its weight is at most $\tau$.

**Policy.** $POL_{its} = \sum_v \omega_v x^v_{its} / \sum_v \omega_v$ over the components observed for the cell, with the component weights in use shown on the Strategic Index page ({{policy_weights}} in this build). An economy is scored when IEA PAMS is observed for at least one of its cells ({{n_policy}} economies; {{n_policy_none}} others have no Policy index and so no Strategic Index); its policy basis names the components its index rests on.

**Strategic Index.** With $POL^*_{its}$ the S-curve of $POL_{its}$ across country $i$'s own in-sample cells,

$$SI_{its} = w_{pol}\,POL^*_{its} + w_{eo}\,EO_{its} + w_{need}\,(1-ES_{its}) + w_{sc}\,\lambda_s + w_{tech}\,g_t ,$$

with the baseline weights $(0.2, 0.3, 0.3, 0.1, 0.1)$ and the stage weights $\lambda_s$ ({{stage_weights}}) at the baseline ({{n_si_cells}} scored cells for {{n_si_countries}} economies in this build). The SI ranks a country's own sectors; it is not designed to compare levels across countries. The sectors the pipeline excludes ({{n_excluded}} in this build: {{excluded_sectors}}) are not reported, and a sector constant across countries (degenerate) is excluded and the index rebuilt, as in the pipeline. The **allied selection** is each coalition member's highest-SI sector at any stage but deployment (ties kept), outside Geothermal and Green Hydrogen and, with the endowment screen on, outside the extraction sectors without a recorded resource base.

**Friendshore and Partnership Opportunity.** Friendshore scores a friendshoring opportunity: where reporter $i$ could build trusted supply in a technology and stage by investing in and sourcing from an aligned partner $j$. It is a weighted mean of $i$'s security need there ($1-ES$), $j$'s Economic Opportunity there (its room to build production and value, not its standing as a supplier today), $i$'s imports from $j$ and $i$'s outward investment in $j$ (and alignment, and in the components and assembly cells of four technologies the Chinese-parent ownership exposure of $j$, where the build has them), less the climate penalty on both sides, then an S-curve across all world dyads of the technology and stage. $i$'s need is the same for every partner in a cell: it sets how much the sector matters to $i$, while the ranking of partners within the cell turns on $j$'s opportunity, the trade and investment ties, alignment and ownership. Friendshore shows where investment and sourcing could go, not today's largest suppliers (the import shares show those). Outward investment is $i$'s IMF direct-investment position in $j$, except at the components and assembly stages of Solar, Wind, Batteries and Electric Vehicles, where it is the tie of parents headquartered in $i$ to their manufacturing projects in $j$ in the Cleantech Manufacturing FDI Dataset (CM-FDI): project count and disclosed capital, ranked within the reporter. In the same cells the exposure $E_{jt}$ is the share of $j$'s foreign-invested manufacturing in technology $t$ whose parent is headquartered in China, Hong Kong or Macao (by disclosed capital where disclosed, by project count otherwise; missing, not zero, below two projects; 1 for China, Hong Kong and Macao themselves). It enters as $1-E_{jt}$ with weight 1, beside alignment at 2: plants in two supplier countries owned by the same Chinese parents do not diversify supply away from China. Partnership Opportunity rates $j$ as a market for $i$'s exports, from $i$'s exports to $j$, $i$'s opportunity and $j$'s security need with weights $(3, 3, 1)$, less the market's climate penalty. Each dyad is summarised by the mean of its five best cells.

**Partnership Strength Index.**

$$PSI_j = \frac{\sum_{k \in K_j} w_k\, C^k_j}{\sum_{k \in K_j} w_k}, \qquad (w_{FS}, w_{PO}, w_{DP}) = (0.4\,m_H,\ 0.4\,m_B,\ 0.2).$$

## Exactness

* At the baseline settings the explorer shows the pipeline's pillars, Policy index, Strategic Index, Friendshore, Opportunity and PSI exactly; the export script checks this every time the data are refreshed, and the result is on the Data page. The checks include each economy's policy basis, the endowment-screen flags of every Strategic Index cell and the allied selection, pick by pick, against the pipeline's table.
* Scenarios are recomputed in your browser by a TypeScript port of the R reference engine (`R/dashboard/`). The port is tested against the R engine on fixed scenarios: pillars, Policy and the Strategic Index agree to within $10^{-9}$, and the Friendshore and Opportunity arrays bit for bit (the inputs are stored losslessly). When the page loads, it also recomputes the baseline and compares it with the exported values; the result is on the Data page.
* Development Potential is held at the pipeline's value under every scenario.
* When you change the ES or EO category weights, the pillars and the Strategic Index are recomputed exactly; unless the build supports it, the partnership measures keep the baseline's pillar-derived components, and the page says so.
* A partnership's rankability uses the imputed share of its best-five cells at the baseline settings; when a scenario changes which cells form the best five, this is an approximation.
