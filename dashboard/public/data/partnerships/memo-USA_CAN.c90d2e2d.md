# United States–Canada Strategic Energy & Industrial Partnership

*Where deeper industrial integration creates strategic value, through which instruments, and where to stay out*

*Generated from Allied Industrial Base Explorer data and a curated external evidence layer (accessed 2026-11-17); evidence, sources and caveats in the appendix. Data as of 17 November 2026.*

## Executive Decision Summary

- **Build together.** Battery Materials & Cells: Battery recycling feed (US->Canada). Nuclear Fuel & Reactor Systems: Uranium conversion & enrichment † (Canada->US).
- **Trade and standards.** Recognise trusted partner supply of nickel intermediates & chemicals; gas assembly; gas refining in procurement and content rules. Keep market access open and predictable for uranium conversion & enrichment †, wind turbine generating sets, electric buses, trucks & tractors (no new tariff on the trusted supplier). Keep transformers; overhead conductors; wind towers open to global sourcing.
- **Institutions.** A new multi-year framework with a shared pipeline, KPIs and review is warranted.
- **Stay out.** Do not co-subsidise new Oil extraction capacity. Do not co-subsidise new Gas components capacity. Do not defend Automatic controllers as a bilateral priority.

**Why this partnership matters.**

- Of 117 technology x stage cells and electro-industrial nodes assessed for the pair (not counting 25 cells whose products are read node by node), 14 show an active complementarity (a supplier meeting the other's need, a shared chokepoint, a capacity-constrained trusted supplier or a chain specialisation), 2 direct competition and 82 no strategic case. The relationship is not one of general similarity: most of the electro-industrial base needs no bilateral intervention. [1]
- AIB strategic roles: US Demand & Market Anchor (primary), Coordination / Bridge Hub (primary), Capital & Investment Anchor (primary), Scale Manufacturing & Integration Platform (secondary), Specialized Technology & Manufacturing Anchor (secondary); Canada Resource & Processing Anchor (primary), Capital & Investment Anchor (secondary), Demand & Market Anchor (secondary). [1]

**The central bilateral bargain.**

- *Inference. *Canada supplies uranium conversion & enrichment †, uranium/fuel extraction: aggregated low-coverage sub-sectors (spent fuel & nuclear scrap, uranium ores) where the US is short; the US supplies battery recycling feed, wind turbine generating sets, refrigerants, electric buses, trucks & tractors where Canada is short; they share no node-level chokepoint where neither supplies; and they specialise at different steps of the same chains in nickel intermediates & chemicals, nuclear special materials, reactor parts.
- *Inference. *From the AIB roles, the US brings demand and offtake, coordination across allied chains, capital; Canada brings resources and processing, capital, demand and offtake. Public finance runs through DOE LPO, DoW OSC, DFC, EXIM on the US side and ISED, EDC, CIB, CGF, CMIF, FinDev on the Canada side. The trade is market access and demand certainty for trusted capacity.

**Do not prioritise** (Section 10):

- *Recommendation. *Do not co-subsidise new Oil extraction capacity: both are strong suppliers (US 11%, Canada 10%). More trusted capacity is needed, so agree who specialises in what rather than racing.
- *Recommendation. *Do not co-subsidise new Gas components capacity: both are strong suppliers (US 21%, Canada 3%). Trusted supply is adequate: this is commercial competition.
- *Recommendation. *Do not defend Automatic controllers as a bilateral priority: trusted supply is adequate (AIB LOW INTERVENTION), yet Canada's observed protection or attention is high.

**Existing mechanisms and the MEA question** (Section 8):

- *Inference. *MEA assessment: **a new MEA-style framework is warranted**. Gaps: no cross-sector KPI dashboard or review cycle; no bilateral mechanism covers SC-NUCLEAR, SC-BATTERIES, SC-GRID, SC-MAGNETS, SC-SOLAR.
- *Recommendation. *Package the compacts into one multi-year framework with a shared pipeline, KPIs and review.

## 1. Current State of the Relationship

This section sets out what already exists before anything new is proposed. Facts are from the curated evidence layer (accessed 2026-11-17); items marked partial or unverified rest on secondary sources and should be confirmed before citation.

**Alliance and plurilateral architecture**

- Canada's memberships in OSI's economic-security agreements table: G7, MSP, NATO, U.S. FTA. [1]
- A joint statement in New York on 23 Sep 2024 set up a network of MSP export-credit agencies, development-finance institutions and state-backed funds. Its purpose is to share information, coordinate and co-finance critical-minerals projects. Named members include DFC, Export Development Canada, JICA, JBIC, KEXIM and Export Finance Norway among others. [2]
- G7 leaders adopted the plan at Kananaskis on 17 Jun 2025, under Canada's presidency. It tasks ministers with a roadmap for standards-based markets with traceability by year-end, calls on export credit agencies, DFIs and MDBs to scale up mining finance, and targets processing, recycling and substitution R&D. Australia, India and Korea later endorsed it. [3]
- Announced in Toronto on 31 Oct 2025, this is the first round of the Production Alliance under the June 2025 G7 Critical Minerals Action Plan. Its 26 investments, partnerships and measures with France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Luxembourg, Norway, the US, Australia and Ukraine aim to unlock C$6.4bn (about US$4.6bn at 0.71 USD/CAD) of graphite, rare-earth and scandium projects. They include Government of Canada offtakes with Nouveau Monde Graphite and Rio Tinto (scandium) and up to C$20.2m for joint R&D. Canada also released a roadmap for standards-based critical-minerals markets. [4]
- Adopted in Toronto on 31 Oct 2025 as an early action under the Action Plan, it aims to reduce and eventually eliminate dependence on supply chains dominated by non-market practices. It prioritises interoperable traceability systems aligned with the UN Transparency Protocol and lists price floors, procurement, tax and trade measures as tools members may use, with no deadlines. [5]
- Carney launched the Alliance at Kananaskis to use strategic investments and offtakes to bring critical-mineral projects into production for defence and advanced technology. At the 30-31 Oct 2025 Toronto ministerial, ministers agreed their critical-minerals envoys would advance the work, and Canada announced investments including offtakes with two Canadian projects. Member list, deal count, values and the US role are not stated in either source. [6]
- At the 19 Feb 2026 Ministerial, IEA ministers endorsed an expanded phase of the Critical Minerals Security Programme, which was established in 2022. The expansion covers: support for members that set up or expand stockpiles; rapid market assessments and coordinated responses during supply disruptions; regular table-top exercises (a rare-earth exercise was held in Nov 2025); a wider information dashboard; a new Technology Collaboration Programme on recycling and substitution. The release gives no budget. [7]
- On 7 Oct 2026 the trade ministers of Argentina, Australia, Canada, the EU, France, Germany, India, Italy, Japan, Korea, Mexico, Poland, Turkiye, the UK and the US signed a statement committing to new sectoral platforms on structural excess capacity. The platforms start with autos and EVs, batteries, chemicals, foundational semiconductors and solar panels, and officials are to meet at technical level before December 2026 to draft terms of reference and share data. [8]
- The Commission and the US launched the pledge at COP26 in 2021 to cut methane emissions 30% by 2030. Canada replaced the US as co-convenor in 2025, and the Commission co-hosted the pledge ministerial at COP31 on 11 Nov 2026. [9]

**Trade architecture and frictions**

- In the OSI energy basket, US imported US$178.8bn from Canada and Canada imported US$61.0bn from US (bilateral flows 2024, summed over the technology x stage cells). [1]
- USMCA entered into force on 1 Jul 2020; under Article 34.7.1 it terminates on 1 Jul 2036 unless each Party confirms it wishes to continue for a new 16-year term. [10]
- The Free Trade Commission held the first joint review on 1 Jul 2026 and the United States did not agree to extend the agreement for a new 16-year term, so the Commission must review it annually until all three Parties confirm extension or the term expires. USTR opened comments for the 2027 annual review on 5 Oct 2026 (deadline 12 Jan 2027), inviting views on North American economic security and non-market practices. [11]
- Invoking Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, the President found Canada's surtax on US vehicles (25% on non-USMCA vehicles and on non-Canadian/Mexican content of USMCA vehicles, plus company-specific quotas) discriminatory and imposed a 50% additional duty on certain Canadian products listed in an annex, effective 19 Aug 2026. The proclamation says US vehicle exports to Canada fell about 22% (US$25.9bn to US$20.3bn) between Apr 2025 and Mar 2026 versus a year earlier. [12]
- The President found that Canada lets retailers use its CETA cheese tariff-rate quota but not its USMCA one, and imposed a 50% additional duty on certain Canadian products under Section 338 from 19 Aug 2026. [13]
- All provinces and territories halted sales of US alcohol from March 2025 (only Alberta and Saskatchewan lifted bans, in June 2025); the proclamation says US alcohol exports to Canada fell about 81% (US$718m to US$137m) and imposed a 50% Section 338 duty on certain Canadian products from 19 Aug 2026. [14]
- The 50% duties were suspended for three days (Proclamation 11056) after Canada committed to remove the measures; the US states Canada reneged and ceased negotiating on 21 Aug 2026, so the duties took effect on 22 Aug. Proclamation 11063 bans imports of certain Canadian products from 29 Sep 2026 (the product annex is not in the text version), and parallel exclusion proclamations for the dairy and alcohol cases were published the same day. [15]
- From 30 Oct 2026 some Canadian products leave and others join the 50% Section 338 duty list (after an earlier rescoping on 8 Sep 2026), and the duties now apply in addition to Section 232 duties; the July proclamation had excluded Section 232 articles. [16]

## 10. What Not to Do

- *Recommendation. ***N01** Do not co-subsidise new Oil extraction capacity: both are strong suppliers (US 11%, Canada 10%). More trusted capacity is needed, so agree who specialises in what rather than racing.
- *Recommendation. ***N02** Do not co-subsidise new Gas components capacity: both are strong suppliers (US 21%, Canada 3%). Trusted supply is adequate: this is commercial competition.
- *Recommendation. ***N03** Do not defend Automatic controllers as a bilateral priority: trusted supply is adequate (AIB LOW INTERVENTION), yet Canada's observed protection or attention is high.
- *Recommendation. ***N04** Do not defend Coal extraction as a bilateral priority: trusted supply is adequate (a large-yard node), yet Canada's observed protection or attention is high.
- *Recommendation. ***N05** Do not defend Semiconductor manufacturing equipment as a bilateral priority: trusted supply is adequate (AIB LOW INTERVENTION), yet US's observed protection or attention is high.
- *Recommendation. ***N06** Do not build a bilateral programme around AC generators: one side is strong but the other has no material need for it; trade it commercially.
