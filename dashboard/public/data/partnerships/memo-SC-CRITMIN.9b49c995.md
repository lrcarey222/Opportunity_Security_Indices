# Critical Minerals Compact

*Where coordinated industrial integration across allied economies creates strategic value, through which instruments, and where to stay out*

*Generated from Allied Industrial Base Explorer data and a curated external evidence layer (accessed 2026-11-17); evidence, sources and caveats in the appendix. Data as of 17 November 2026.*

## Executive Decision Summary

- **Build together.** Metals & engineered materials: Synthetic graphite (anode-grade). Refined chemicals & oxides: Rare-earth oxides & compounds. Refined chemicals & oxides: Cobalt intermediates & chemicals. Ores & concentrates: Natural graphite. Refined chemicals & oxides: Manganese dioxide (EMD).
- **Trade and standards.** Recognise member supply of synthetic graphite (anode-grade), rare-earth oxides & compounds, natural graphite and 7 others in procurement and content rules. Bind market access among members for cobalt intermediates & chemicals and nickel intermediates & chemicals.
- **Institutions.** Give Minerals Security Partnership and its successor FORGE a compact mandate: pipeline, reciprocal commitments, KPIs and an annual review. No new body.
- **Stay out.** Do not co-subsidise new PGMs (iridium, ruthenium) capacity among members. Do not run parallel national subsidy races for copper ores & concentrates. Do not create a new compact secretariat or treaty body.

**Why this compact matters.**

- Of 16 electro-industrial nodes in the compact, 15 show an active compact relation (member supply meeting member need, a capacity-constrained trusted supplier or a shared chokepoint), 1 member competition and 0 no strategic case. Nearly every step is under stress, so a compact is the right scale of response; the rules then rank where it acts first (8 nodes need new capacity, the rest coordinated trade). [1]
- A coercive supplier holds at least 50% of world supply at 8 nodes: synthetic graphite (anode-grade) (CHN 94%); rare-earth oxides & compounds (CHN 84%); cobalt intermediates & chemicals (CHN 76%); natural graphite (CHN 82%); rare-earth ores (monazite) (CHN 59%); rare-earth metals (CHN 84%); lithium chemicals (CHN 74%); copper smelting & refining (CHN 50%). [1]
- AIB node classes under the China shock: BUILD 8, INSUFFICIENT DATA 2, LOW INTERVENTION 6; allied demand across the compact's nodes US$115.7bn. [1]
- 43 member economies; core (AIB): Chile (resource supplier (SC-GRID)); Japan (specialized technology supplier (SC-GRID)); DR Congo (processing anchor (SC-GRID)); United States (resource supplier (SC-BATTERIES)); Korea (specialized technology supplier (SC-SEMIS)); India (processing anchor (SC-INDUSTRY)); Peru (resource supplier (SC-GRID)); Germany (demand anchor (SC-BATTERIES)). [1]

**The central compact bargain.**

- *Inference. *By 2024 trade, the largest member suppliers (Chile, Peru, Indonesia, Australia and 14 others) supply synthetic graphite (anode-grade), rare-earth oxides & compounds, cobalt intermediates & chemicals, natural graphite, manganese dioxide (EMD) and 9 others, and the largest member buyers are Japan, United States, Korea, Brazil and 13 others; no node is a shared vulnerability that no member can supply.
- *Inference. *The trade is market access and demand certainty for trusted capacity: suppliers commit capacity and priority supply, buyers commit offtake and procurement recognition, and the compact keeps the remaining nodes open.

**Do not prioritise** (Section 10):

- *Recommendation. *Do not co-subsidise new PGMs (iridium, ruthenium) capacity among members: Germany and South Africa are already strong suppliers. Trusted supply is adequate: this is commercial competition.
- *Recommendation. *Do not run parallel national subsidy races for copper ores & concentrates: 3 members (Chile, DR Congo and Peru) are already strong suppliers; agree in the compact who expands where.
- *Recommendation. *Do not create a new compact secretariat or treaty body: Minerals Security Partnership and its successor FORGE already exists (partial finance and projects but no published list); dock the compact in it.

**Existing mechanisms and the governance question** (Section 8):

- *Inference. *Governance assessment: **upgrade an existing plurilateral mechanism (CCRM-M1)**. Gaps: CCRM-M1 has no published project pipeline; CCRM-M1 has no dedicated finance; CCRM-M1 has no KPI dashboard or review cycle; core members outside CCRM-M1: CHL, COD, PER.
- *Recommendation. *Do not create a new compact body. Give Minerals Security Partnership and its successor FORGE a compact mandate for these nodes: a shared project pipeline, reciprocal supplier and buyer commitments and a KPI dashboard, reviewed every 12 months.

## 1. Current State of the Compact

This section sets out what already exists before anything new is proposed. Facts are from the curated evidence layer (accessed 2026-11-17); items marked partial rest on secondary sources and should be confirmed before citation; unverified items are left out here and stay in the evidence register.

**Alliance and plurilateral architecture**

- Members in OSI's economic-security agreements table: G7 8 (CAN, DEU, EUU, FRA, GBR, ITA, JPN and USA); IPEF 11 (AUS, IDN, IND, JPN, KOR, MYS, PHL, SGP, THA, USA and VNM); MSP 14 (AUS, CAN, DEU, EUU, FIN, FRA, GBR, IND, ITA, JPN, KOR, NOR and 2 others); MSP Forum 7 (ARG, COD, MEX, NAM, PER, PHL and TUR); NATO 17 (BEL, BGR, CAN, DEU, ESP, FIN, FRA, GBR, GRC, HUN, ITA, NLD and 5 others); U.S. FTA 9 (AUS, CAN, CHL, COL, KOR, MAR, MEX, PER and SGP). [1]
- G7 leaders adopted the plan at Kananaskis on 17 Jun 2025, under Canada's presidency. It tasks ministers with a roadmap for standards-based markets with traceability by year-end, calls on export credit agencies, DFIs and MDBs to scale up mining finance, and targets processing, recycling and substitution R&D. Australia, India and Korea later endorsed it. [2]
- Announced in Toronto on 31 Oct 2025, this is the first round of the Production Alliance under the June 2025 G7 Critical Minerals Action Plan. Its 26 investments, partnerships and measures with France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Luxembourg, Norway, the US, Australia and Ukraine aim to unlock C$6.4bn (about US$4.6bn at 0.71 USD/CAD) of graphite, rare-earth and scandium projects. They include Government of Canada offtakes with Nouveau Monde Graphite and Rio Tinto (scandium) and up to C$20.2m for joint R&D. Canada also released a roadmap for standards-based critical-minerals markets. [3]
- Carney launched the Alliance at Kananaskis to use strategic investments and offtakes to bring critical-mineral projects into production for defence and advanced technology. At the 30-31 Oct 2025 Toronto ministerial, ministers agreed their critical-minerals envoys would advance the work, and Canada announced investments including offtakes with two Canadian projects. Member list, deal count, values and the US role are not stated in either source. [4]
- The second round discussed a joint response to falling copper treatment and refining charges, cooperation within the Minerals Security Partnership and ways to develop critical minerals jointly in third countries; no agreement or named project was reported. [5]
- The 4 Feb 2026 Critical Minerals Ministerial at the State Department drew 55 foreign delegations and produced 13 bilateral critical-minerals frameworks or MOUs. These include a framework with Argentina, an agreement with the UK and a US-Mexico action plan proposing border-adjusted price floors, with details due within 60 days; the source does not name the other signatories. Vice President Vance proposed a plurilateral preferential trade zone with reference prices at each production stage, enforced by adjustable tariffs, and FORGE replaced the MSP. [6]
- At the 19 Feb 2026 Ministerial, IEA ministers endorsed an expanded phase of the Critical Minerals Security Programme, which was established in 2022. The expansion covers: support for members that set up or expand stockpiles; rapid market assessments and coordinated responses during supply disruptions; regular table-top exercises (a rare-earth exercise was held in Nov 2025); a wider information dashboard; a new Technology Collaboration Programme on recycling and substitution. The release gives no budget. [7]
- At PDAC on 2 Mar 2026 Canada announced a second round of 30 partnerships and investments with 12 allied partners, covering C$12.1bn (about US$8.6bn at 0.71 USD/CAD) of projects. This brings the Alliance total to C$18.5bn of Canadian critical-minerals projects. The release does not name the partners or the projects. [8]
- Quad foreign ministers adopted the Quad Critical Minerals Initiative Framework in New Delhi on 26 May 2026, under which the four intend to mobilise up to US$20bn in government and private-sector support through new and existing efforts for mining, processing and recycling, by identifying projects with a Quad nexus (located in, operated by companies headquartered in, or supplying Quad partners) and backing them through ECAs, DFIs, guarantees, loans, equity, insurance, subsidies and offtake. No project list or split by partner has been published; the only new money named by 31 Oct 2026 was a US$10m US commitment for critical-mineral recovery from waste. [9]
- The USSC, in partnership with the US Department of State, is convening two expert workshops in 2026 on how the four can operationalise the 2025 initiative and May 2026 framework: the first was held in Feb 2026, the second was scheduled for Oct 2026, and a final outcomes report is due in Jan 2027. The July 2026 report sets out policy options and describes the framework's goal of mobilising up to US$20bn as an objective to mobilise government and private capital. [10]
- VaultCo said on 23 Sep 2026 that Swiss-based traders Glencore and Mercuria had each committed US$500m, and the reserve moved into its operating phase. It is separate from the DLA-run National Defense Stockpile. No mineral purchases, EXIM loan closing, purchase-pricing rules or price-floor mechanism had been disclosed by mid-October 2026. The reported scope is the 60-mineral USGS list, with initial emphasis on rare earths, cobalt, nickel, lithium, copper, gallium, germanium and scandium. [11]

**Market structure**

- Excluding rare earths, the top refining country's average share of key energy minerals rose to 72% in 2025 from 70% in 2023. China is the top refiner of each key energy mineral except nickel, where Indonesia leads. The number of mineral tariff codes under Chinese export controls has tripled since 2023. Full implementation of the expanded rare-earth controls could put about US$6.5 trillion a year of downstream production outside China at risk; a full disruption of battery-grade graphite trade would put over US$300bn a year at risk. [12]
- New refining projects in the US and Malaysia cut China's share of rare-earth refining to about 85% in 2025 from 90% in 2023; it could fall to about 70% by 2035 if all planned projects proceed (IEA figures as reported by Reuters). The executive summary adds that diversified refining capacity by 2035 equals about two-thirds of expected mined supply, while planned magnet production equals only about one-third. Diversifying magnet rare-earth supply would need about US$60bn of investment over a decade. [13]
- In FY2024-25 India imported 2.7 Mt of critical minerals worth about US$12bn, of which Australia supplied about 6% by volume and value, mainly copper ores and concentrates and nickel oxides and hydroxides (65% of India's imports of the latter). Australian lithium barely features in India's lithium imports, much of which is processed in China and Europe. [14]
- Wesfarmers' 2026 Annual Report (year to 30 Jun 2026) says the Covalent Lithium Kwinana refinery, a Wesfarmers-SQM joint venture, achieved first lithium hydroxide production and sales in FY2026 and is still ramping up. Production rates are expected to accelerate through January-June 2027 as odour mitigation is put in place, while product qualification with key offtake partners continues. No US offtaker or investor is named. [15]
- At the 2 Sep 2026 AKBC-KABC meeting in Adelaide POSCO's chair said Australia supplies about 70% of the group's raw materials, as it has since 1971; POSCO also owns Senex Energy (2022); no new agreements were signed. [16]
- The Matawinie mine has been under construction since 2026 (C$67m spent by 30 Sep 2026; mining lease granted Oct 2026), and the 13 ktpy Becancour anode plant is targeting FID in the coming months. Offtakers for about 70% of output are Panasonic Energy, Traxys and the Government of Canada, and the Canada Growth Fund committed about US$82m in Apr 2026; no US buyer or US government finance is named. [17]

**Trade measures, controls and frictions**

- Signed 28 Mar 2023 and in force on signature without congressional approval; covers cobalt/graphite/lithium/manganese/nickel; no export duties; consult on non-market measures and forced-labour imports; Treasury/USTR treated it as an FTA for the IRA EV credit critical-minerals test. [18]
- Albanese and Trump signed the US-Australia Framework for Securing of Supply in the Mining and Processing of Critical Minerals and Rare Earths in Washington on 20 Oct 2025. Each side is to provide at least US$1bn within six months toward an US$8.5bn pipeline of priority projects, and a Supply Security Response Group led by the US Energy Secretary and the Australian Resources Minister is to be set up. On 29 Oct 2025 its trade minister said the January 2024 ban on raw rare-earth exports remains, so rare earths must be processed in Malaysia before export. [21]
- The White House says China will suspend the global implementation of the export controls it announced on 9 Oct 2025. It also says China will issue general licences for exports of rare earths, gallium, germanium, antimony and graphite to US end users and their suppliers worldwide, which it calls the de facto removal of controls China has imposed since 2023. [22]
- The final 2025 list, published on 7 Nov 2025, has 60 minerals, up from 50 in 2022. It adds boron, copper, lead, metallurgical coal, phosphate, potash, rhenium, silicon, silver and uranium. The list is to be updated not less than every two years. [23]
- On 9 Nov 2025 MOFCOM suspended clause 2 of Announcement No. 46 of 2024 until 27 Nov 2026. That clause barred, in principle, exports to the US of gallium, germanium, antimony and superhard-material dual-use items, and subjected graphite exports to stricter end-user and end-use checks; licensing still applies. No extension of this suspension was reported as of 13 Nov 2026. [24]
- The 14 Jan 2026 Section 232 proclamation imposes no tariffs. It directs Commerce and USTR to negotiate agreements with trading partners and to promote price floors for trade in processed critical minerals and derivative products. Further import measures may follow if agreements are not reached within 180 days (about 13 Jul 2026), are not carried out or prove ineffective. The fact sheet cites no import-dependence figures. [25]
- On 31 Oct 2025 Canada said it would use the Defence Production Act to stockpile critical minerals. The stated purposes are domestic and allied defence supply, Arctic sovereignty and NATO goals. The Defence Industrial Strategy of 17 Feb 2026 allows stockpiling of critical and dual-use minerals. Volumes and budget are not stated. [26]
- Proclamation 10962 (30 Jul 2025) imposed a 50% tariff on the copper content of semi-finished copper products and intensive derivatives from 1 Aug 2025. It does not stack on Section 232 auto tariffs. A refined-copper duty of 15% from 1 Jan 2027 and 30% from 1 Jan 2028 depended on a Commerce market update due 30 Jun 2026, and no decision on it was reported. [27]
- Proclamation 11021 (2 Apr 2026) set full-value Section 232 duties of 50% on steel, aluminium and certain copper articles, with lower UK rates only. For the Annex I-C derivative list, Proclamation 11032 charges USMCA-qualifying Canadian and Mexican products 25% on non-US content only (floor 15%), while Japan, Korea, the EU, the UK and others receive a 15% all-in cap that Canada does not. [28]
- On 9 Oct 2026 the Commission selected 46 strategic projects in 16 member states: 7 on lithium, 12 nickel, 10 cobalt, 5 manganese, 4 graphite and 4 rare earths. They add to 47 EU and 13 third-country projects chosen in 2025. Since the Dec 2025 RESourceEU communication, Team Europe has committed over EUR 2bn through the Innovation Fund, state aid and the EIB, and the Raw Materials Mechanism has made over 30 matches for 10 materials. No US company or US-located project is named. [29]
- Announcement 2026 No. 58 of 9 Nov 2026 keeps MOFCOM/GAC Announcements 2025 Nos. 55, 56, 57 and 58 and MOFCOM Announcements 2025 Nos. 61 and 62 suspended from 11 Nov 2026 to 10 Jan 2027. These are the October 2025 controls on superhard materials, rare-earth equipment, further medium and heavy rare earths, lithium-battery and graphite-anode items, the extraterritorial rare-earth rule and rare-earth technology. The extension adds two months to the Busan-truce suspension, which was due to lapse on 10 Nov 2026. China's April 2025 licensing of seven medium and heavy rare earths (UJ-024) is not part of the suspension. [30]

## 10. What Not to Do

- *Recommendation. ***N01** Do not co-subsidise new PGMs (iridium, ruthenium) capacity among members: Germany and South Africa are already strong suppliers. Trusted supply is adequate: this is commercial competition.
- *Recommendation. ***N02** Do not run parallel national subsidy races for copper ores & concentrates: 3 members (Chile, DR Congo and Peru) are already strong suppliers; agree in the compact who expands where.
- *Recommendation. ***N03** Do not create a new compact secretariat or treaty body: Minerals Security Partnership and its successor FORGE already exists (partial finance and projects but no published list); dock the compact in it.
- *Recommendation. ***N04** Do not put new public money behind battery recycling feed projects that are already committed (evidence CBAT-24): member suppliers already serve member buyers, so compact support is additional only beyond them.
- *Recommendation. ***N05** Do not put new public money behind copper ores & concentrates projects that are already committed (evidence USCA-19, CBAT-08, INAU-21): member suppliers already serve member buyers, so compact support is additional only beyond them.
- *Recommendation. ***N06** Do not put new public money behind copper smelting & refining projects that are already committed (evidence USDE-22, INAU-16): member suppliers already serve member buyers, so compact support is additional only beyond them.
