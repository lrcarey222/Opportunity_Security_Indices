# United States–European Union Strategic Energy & Industrial Partnership

*Where deeper industrial integration creates strategic value, through which instruments, and where to stay out*

*Generated from Allied Industrial Base Explorer data and a curated external evidence layer (accessed 2026-11-17); evidence, sources and caveats in the appendix. Data as of 17 November 2026.*

## Executive Decision Summary

- **Build together.** Battery Materials & Cells: Synthetic graphite (anode-grade) † (EU->US); Natural graphite (joint); Cobalt intermediates & chemicals (EU->US); Other accumulators (joint). Permanent Magnets & Drive Systems: Rare-earth oxides & compounds (joint); Ferrite & other magnets (EU->US). Electric Vehicles: Electric & plug-in cars (EU->US). Heat Pumps & Cooling: Refrigerants (US->EU). Wind & Hydropower Equipment: Wind blades & hubs (EU->US).
- **Trade and standards.** Recognise trusted partner supply of electric & plug-in cars; ferrite & other magnets; refrigerants in procurement and content rules. Common anti-circumvention rules for rare-earth oxides & compounds and li-ion cells & packs. Keep market access open and predictable for synthetic graphite (anode-grade) †, cobalt intermediates & chemicals, wind blades & hubs, nuclear reactors & enrichment machinery † (no new tariff on the trusted supplier). Keep nuclear special materials; transformers; overhead conductors open to global sourcing.
- **Institutions.** A new multi-year framework with a shared pipeline, KPIs and review is warranted.
- **Stay out.** Do not co-subsidise new Electric buses, trucks & tractors capacity. Do not co-subsidise new AC generators capacity. Do not co-subsidise new Automatic controllers capacity.

**Why this partnership matters.**

- Of 117 technology x stage cells and electro-industrial nodes assessed for the pair (not counting 25 cells whose products are read node by node), 35 show an active complementarity (a supplier meeting the other's need, a shared chokepoint, a capacity-constrained trusted supplier or a chain specialisation), 12 direct competition and 49 no strategic case. The relationship is not one of general similarity: most of the electro-industrial base needs no bilateral intervention. [1]
- AIB strategic roles: US Demand & Market Anchor (primary), Coordination / Bridge Hub (primary), Capital & Investment Anchor (primary), Scale Manufacturing & Integration Platform (secondary), Specialized Technology & Manufacturing Anchor (secondary); EU Capital & Investment Anchor (primary), Scale Manufacturing & Integration Platform (primary), Demand & Market Anchor (primary), Coordination / Bridge Hub (secondary), Specialized Technology & Manufacturing Anchor (secondary). **The central bilateral bargain.**

- *Inference. *The EU supplies synthetic graphite (anode-grade) †, cobalt intermediates & chemicals, wind blades & hubs, electric & plug-in cars where the US is short; the US supplies refrigerants where the EU is short; the two share exposure to coercive suppliers in rare-earth oxides & compounds, natural graphite, other accumulators, li-ion cells & packs, copper smelting & refining, where neither can supply the other and the answer is joint demand, finance and third-country capacity; and they specialise at different steps of the same chains in silver powder, electric rail vehicles.
- *Inference. *From the AIB roles, the US brings demand and offtake, coordination across allied chains, capital; the EU brings capital, scale manufacturing, demand and offtake. Public finance runs through DOE LPO, DoW OSC, DFC, EXIM on the US side and EIB, Innovation Fund, EHB, Global Gateway, RESourceEU, IPCEI on the EU side. The trade is market access and demand certainty for trusted capacity.

## 1. Current State of the Relationship

This section sets out what already exists before anything new is proposed. Facts are from the curated evidence layer (accessed 2026-11-17); items marked partial or unverified rest on secondary sources and should be confirmed before citation.

**Alliance and plurilateral architecture**

- EU's memberships in OSI's economic-security agreements table: G7, MSP. [1]
- G7 leaders adopted the plan at Kananaskis on 17 Jun 2025, under Canada's presidency. It tasks ministers with a roadmap for standards-based markets with traceability by year-end, calls on export credit agencies, DFIs and MDBs to scale up mining finance, and targets processing, recycling and substitution R&D. Australia, India and Korea later endorsed it. [2]
- Announced in Toronto on 31 Oct 2025, this is the first round of the Production Alliance under the June 2025 G7 Critical Minerals Action Plan. Its 26 investments, partnerships and measures with France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Luxembourg, Norway, the US, Australia and Ukraine aim to unlock C$6.4bn (about US$4.6bn at 0.71 USD/CAD) of graphite, rare-earth and scandium projects. They include Government of Canada offtakes with Nouveau Monde Graphite and Rio Tinto (scandium) and up to C$20.2m for joint R&D. Canada also released a roadmap for standards-based critical-minerals markets. [3]
- Adopted in Toronto on 31 Oct 2025 as an early action under the Action Plan, it aims to reduce and eventually eliminate dependence on supply chains dominated by non-market practices. It prioritises interoperable traceability systems aligned with the UN Transparency Protocol and lists price floors, procurement, tax and trade measures as tools members may use, with no deadlines. [4]
- Carney launched the Alliance at Kananaskis to use strategic investments and offtakes to bring critical-mineral projects into production for defence and advanced technology. At the 30-31 Oct 2025 Toronto ministerial, ministers agreed their critical-minerals envoys would advance the work, and Canada announced investments including offtakes with two Canadian projects. Member list, deal count, values and the US role are not stated in either source. [5]
- At the 19 Feb 2026 Ministerial, IEA ministers endorsed an expanded phase of the Critical Minerals Security Programme, which was established in 2022. The expansion covers: support for members that set up or expand stockpiles; rapid market assessments and coordinated responses during supply disruptions; regular table-top exercises (a rare-earth exercise was held in Nov 2025); a wider information dashboard; a new Technology Collaboration Programme on recycling and substitution. The release gives no budget. [6]
- In Aug 2025 the EU and US said they intend to ring-fence their markets from steel and aluminium overcapacity, including through a tariff-rate-quota solution for EU exports of steel, aluminium and their derivatives (Joint Statement para 3). The Commission's 20 May 2026 release still lists steel overcapacity as continuing work and announces no quota, so EU metals remain under the Section 232 tariffs (see USDE-07). [7]
- On 7 Oct 2026 the trade ministers of Argentina, Australia, Canada, the EU, France, Germany, India, Italy, Japan, Korea, Mexico, Poland, Turkiye, the UK and the US signed a statement committing to new sectoral platforms on structural excess capacity. The platforms start with autos and EVs, batteries, chemicals, foundational semiconductors and solar panels, and officials are to meet at technical level before December 2026 to draft terms of reference and share data. [8]
- The Commission and the US launched the pledge at COP26 in 2021 to cut methane emissions 30% by 2030. Canada replaced the US as co-convenor in 2025, and the Commission co-hosted the pledge ministerial at COP31 on 11 Nov 2026. [9]
