# Japan–Korea Strategic Energy & Industrial Partnership

*Where deeper industrial integration creates strategic value, through which instruments, and where to stay out*

*Generated from Allied Industrial Base Explorer data and a curated external evidence layer (accessed 2026-11-17); evidence, sources and caveats in the appendix. Data as of 17 November 2026.*

## Executive Decision Summary

- **Build together.** Solar Manufacturing: Wafers † (Japan->Korea); Polysilicon † (joint). Permanent Magnets & Drive Systems: Rare-earth oxides & compounds (joint); Rare-earth permanent magnets (Japan->Korea). Battery Materials & Cells: Natural graphite (joint); Cobalt intermediates & chemicals (joint); Lithium chemicals (joint); Battery materials (foil, electrolyte) (Japan->Korea).
- **Trade and standards.** Recognise trusted partner supply of battery materials (foil, electrolyte); solar cells in procurement and content rules. Common anti-circumvention rules for rare-earth oxides & compounds and cobalt intermediates & chemicals and lithium chemicals. Keep market access open and predictable for wafers †, rare-earth permanent magnets (no new tariff on the trusted supplier). Keep PGMs (iridium, ruthenium); copper ores & concentrates; nickel intermediates & chemicals open to global sourcing.
- **Institutions.** Upgrade Korea-Japan Supply Chain Partnership Arrangement and Industrial and Trade Policy Dialogue: sector strategy, reciprocal commitments, a KPI dashboard and an annual review. No new MOU.
- **Stay out.** Do not co-subsidise new Accumulator parts & separators capacity. Do not create a new bilateral energy MOU or a free-standing MEA institution. Do not put new public money behind Integrated circuits investment that is already committed (evidence JPKR-31).

**Why this partnership matters.**

- Of 117 technology x stage cells and electro-industrial nodes assessed for the pair (not counting 25 cells whose products are read node by node), 23 show an active complementarity (a supplier meeting the other's need, a shared chokepoint, a capacity-constrained trusted supplier or a chain specialisation), 1 direct competition and 69 no strategic case. The relationship is not one of general similarity: most of the electro-industrial base needs no bilateral intervention. [1]
- AIB strategic roles: Japan Capital & Investment Anchor (primary), Specialized Technology & Manufacturing Anchor (primary), Scale Manufacturing & Integration Platform (primary), Coordination / Bridge Hub (secondary); Korea Specialized Technology & Manufacturing Anchor (primary), Scale Manufacturing & Integration Platform (primary), Coordination / Bridge Hub (secondary), Capital & Investment Anchor (secondary). [1]

**The central bilateral bargain.**

- *Inference. *Korea supplies gallium, germanium & indium where Japan is short; Japan supplies wafers †, rare-earth permanent magnets, battery materials (foil, electrolyte) where Korea is short; the two share exposure to coercive suppliers in polysilicon †, rare-earth oxides & compounds, natural graphite, cobalt intermediates & chemicals, lithium chemicals, where neither can supply the other and the answer is joint demand, finance and third-country capacity; and they specialise at different steps of the same chains in solar cells, cathode materials & precursors, piezo-electric quartz, manganese dioxide (EMD) †.
- *Inference. *From the AIB roles, Japan brings capital, specialised technology and manufacturing, scale manufacturing; Korea brings specialised technology and manufacturing, scale manufacturing, coordination across allied chains. Public finance runs through JBIC, NEXI, JOGMEC on the Japan side and KEXIM, K-SURE, KOMIR, KDB on the Korea side. The trade is market access and demand certainty for trusted capacity.

## 1. Current State of the Relationship

This section sets out what already exists before anything new is proposed. Facts are from the curated evidence layer (accessed 2026-11-17); items marked partial or unverified rest on secondary sources and should be confirmed before citation.

**Alliance and plurilateral architecture**

- Korea's memberships in OSI's economic-security agreements table: IPEF, MSP, U.S. FTA. [1]
- DFAT notes RCEP entered into force for Korea on 1 Feb 2022 and may be used by exporters as an alternative to KAFTA. [2]
- The 16 Mar 2023 Tokyo summit restored shuttle diplomacy, with Japan pledging to lift its three-material curbs and Korea to drop its WTO case. [3]
- A joint statement in New York on 23 Sep 2024 set up a network of MSP export-credit agencies, development-finance institutions and state-backed funds. Its purpose is to share information, coordinate and co-finance critical-minerals projects. Named members include DFC, Export Development Canada, JICA, JBIC, KEXIM and Export Finance Norway among others. [4]
- Korean and Japanese officials agreed a close cooperation system on critical-mineral supply risks, support for company and agency cooperation on joint development in third countries (Latin America, Asia, Africa), and policy coordination in the Minerals Security Partnership and IEA; no projects were named. [5]
- The dialogue, set up Feb 2024 with a first meeting in Jun 2024, held its second meeting in Tokyo on 26 Mar 2025 and agreed to develop a Japan-ROK cooperation platform for hydrogen and ammonia supply chains. [6]
- Senior officials meeting in New York agreed to enhance dialogue on critical minerals and cooperation in Southeast Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa through the trilateral Early Warning System. [7]
- At the October 2025 Tokyo conference the two business federations agreed Korea's CPTPP participation was important given US protectionism and supply-chain disruption. [8]
- The second round discussed a joint response to falling copper treatment and refining charges, cooperation within the Minerals Security Partnership and ways to develop critical minerals jointly in third countries; no agreement or named project was reported. [9]
- On 13 Jan 2026 the leaders discussed supply-chain cooperation in depth and agreed to launch agency-level talks on economic security cooperation, with rare-earth stabilisation reported as a focus; no outputs were published. [10]
- The US-hosted ministerial in Feb 2026 was attended by Japan and Korea among others, and Korea was reported to chair the FORGE critical-minerals forum through June 2026. [11]
- Korea chaired the Minerals Security Partnership until June 2026; a February 2026 report says its 17 members carried into the successor FORGE; Korean public lenders joined the MSP Finance Network; no signed US-Korea critical-minerals MOU was found. [12]
- At the 19 Feb 2026 Ministerial, IEA ministers endorsed an expanded phase of the Critical Minerals Security Programme, which was established in 2022. The expansion covers: support for members that set up or expand stockpiles; rapid market assessments and coordinated responses during supply disruptions; regular table-top exercises (a rare-earth exercise was held in Nov 2025); a wider information dashboard; a new Technology Collaboration Programme on recycling and substitution. The release gives no budget. [13]
- A standing consultative body launched 14 Mar 2026 to review trade cooperation, economic security, supply chains, steel and mineral resources; the same article reports plans for joint exploration and investment in critical minerals and shared market monitoring; no schedule published. [14]
- The 19 May 2026 trade-ministry release covers crude oil swaps and mutual supply in a crisis, deeper LNG cooperation under the KOGAS-JERA agreement, SCPA crisis mechanisms and regional reserves via Japan's proposed POWERR Asia; no projects or funding named; hydrogen and critical minerals not covered. [15]
- In June 2026 Korea's trade ministry said no decision had been made on applying to CPTPP after reports of a late-June application; Japan reportedly would back accession without first requiring Korea to lift its Fukushima seafood ban; the Japan position is a press report. [16]
- On 7 Oct 2026 the trade ministers of Argentina, Australia, Canada, the EU, France, Germany, India, Italy, Japan, Korea, Mexico, Poland, Turkiye, the UK and the US signed a statement committing to new sectoral platforms on structural excess capacity. The platforms start with autos and EVs, batteries, chemicals, foundational semiconductors and solar panels, and officials are to meet at technical level before December 2026 to draft terms of reference and share data. [17]
- The joint statement backs coordination on resource procurement and supply-chain stability, support for Korea's CPTPP accession, hydrogen certification and safety standards, critical-mineral stockpiling and crisis response through the SCPA, and an early resumption of the Korea-US-Japan Business Dialogue; business statement, not binding. [18]

**Trade architecture and frictions**

- In the OSI energy basket, Japan imported US$10.2bn from Korea and Korea imported US$10.7bn from Japan (bilateral flows 2024, summed over the technology x stage cells). [1]
- RCEP entered into force for Japan on 1 Jan 2022 and for Korea on 1 Feb 2022, giving the two countries a shared preferential trade framework with no separate bilateral FTA; tariff schedules not reviewed. [19]
- From July 2019 Japan required individual licences for fluorinated polyimide, photoresist and hydrogen fluoride exports to Korea and removed Korea from its white list; Korea removed Japan from its own list in Aug 2019 and filed a WTO complaint; Japan lifted the three-item curbs in Mar 2023. [20]
- Korea completed procedures to drop its 2019 WTO complaint on 23 Mar 2023, a week after the 16 Mar Yoon-Kishida summit; Japan had pledged to lift the three-material curbs; the article does not confirm the curbs had by then been lifted. [21]

**Industrial and energy agreements**

- The three leaders agreed to pilot an early warning system exchanging supply-chain information via embassies in selected countries, with critical minerals and rechargeable batteries as priority products, plus an annual commerce and industry ministers track; the system is not published as operating beyond the pilot. [22]
- KNOC reportedly signed an MOU with JOGMEC in February 2025 on cooperation in hydrogen, ammonia and CCS. [23]
- Ministers Kim Jung-kwan and Akazawa signed the SCPA in Tokyo on 14 Mar 2026: early notification of disruptions, an emergency meeting within five days of a request, and prior consultation before measures that could harm the other side; no amounts or named projects. [24]

**Corporate relationships in the recommended compacts**

## 10. What Not to Do

- *Recommendation. ***N01** Do not co-subsidise new Accumulator parts & separators capacity: both are strong suppliers (Japan 11%, Korea 13%). More trusted capacity is needed, so agree who specialises in what rather than racing.
- *Recommendation. ***N02** Do not create a new bilateral energy MOU or a free-standing MEA institution: JPKR-M1 already exists (a standing framework with no pipeline or finance yet); dock the compacts in it.
- *Recommendation. ***N03** Do not put new public money behind Integrated circuits investment that is already committed (evidence JPKR-31); support is additional only beyond it.
- *Recommendation. ***N04** Do not defend Coal extraction as a bilateral priority: trusted supply is adequate (a large-yard node), yet Korea's observed protection or attention is high.
- *Recommendation. ***N05** Do not defend Gas refining as a bilateral priority: trusted supply is adequate (a large-yard node), yet Korea's observed protection or attention is high.

**Where the findings are weak or counterintuitive**

- Evidence-led compacts (OSI cannot see the partner's capability in trade data): Semiconductors & Power Electronics; Industrial Electrification & Hydrogen.

Trade data miss capability that is owned abroad or sold at home, so these nodes cannot show complementarity even where corporate evidence suggests it.
- Product-definition caveats (†) at 3 nodes: the notes are under the Section 2 node table.
- Low-evidence units among the active states: none.
- Trade data understate concentration at uranium conversion & enrichment: enrichment is sold as a service under long-term contracts, so goods trade understates Russia's share of world enrichment capacity (roughly 40%), and the 2024 US ban on Russian low-enriched uranium imports makes trusted capacity the binding constraint.
- Trade data understate concentration at gallium, germanium & indium: China's export controls since 2023 cut its recorded exports, so 2024 trade understates its share of primary gallium and germanium output (the large majority of world supply).
- Fuel-trade lanes reach tier 1 (coal refining; oil components; coal components; buyer: both and Korea and Japan): real energy-security lanes, but outside the electro-industrial compacts and with no market failure beyond the supply concentration the engine measures.
