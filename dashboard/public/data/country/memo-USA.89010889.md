# The United States and the Allied Industrial Base

*What to secure, build, source from partners, and deploy*

*Generated from Allied Industrial Base Explorer data; evidence, sources and caveats in the appendix. Data as of 26 November 2026.*

## 1. Bottom line — the United States' strategic role

In the Allied Industrial Base the United States is the demand and market anchor, with a core seat in all ten strategic compacts. It also supplies fab equipment, automatic controllers and hydrogen, ammonia and methanol, and it is the largest allied holder of gas liquids, refined fuels and gas-turbine and drilling components. Nationally, its task is to keep the broad manufacturing base where it holds a top-quarter position, from battery cells, EV drivetrains and reactors to large power transformers, wind turbines and PV modules. It should catch up in battery materials refining, its best-anchored gap. The fence that matters is narrow: the rare-earth chain, the electrical steel inside transformers, and the control software of the battery systems it imports mostly from China. It should source battery and EV minerals from trusted producers, and use development cooperation to add trusted capacity in EV drivetrains and battery cells. Much of the map is left open: mining, fuels and basic processing are large, commoditized yards, and only battery storage and gas networks clear the bar for faster deployment. [1, 2]

- **Secure.** The rare-earth chain from refining to traction magnets, where China holds 94% of world refining supply; grain-oriented electrical steel for transformers; and the firmware and communications of imported battery systems. The answer is qualified trusted supply, stocks and control of command paths, not self-sufficiency. [2]
- **Build / preserve.** Preserve the top-quarter positions in battery cells and components, EV drivetrains and vehicles, reactors and nuclear fuel, large power transformers, wind turbines and their parts, PV modules and inverters, and gas and oil equipment. Catch Up in battery materials refining.
- **Partner.** Source battery and EV minerals from Australia and Canada, where allied supply covers the need, and the wider wind-materials refining step from the allied holders Germany, Belgium and Spain. Allied supply of that step falls short of need, so new capacity is also required. With developing partners, development finance and offtake can add capacity in battery cells and, over a longer horizon, in rare-earth and wind materials. [1, 2, 3]
- **Deploy / stay open.** Accelerate battery storage and gas networks, with the market open to every trusted supplier. Keep the Watch mining steps, fuels processing and grid components open. Keep supply reliable in gas liquids, which allies rely on most, and in refined fuels, which they rely on less.

Mining, processing and deployment are mostly Watch, and the few Catch Up calls sit in refining and recycling. The orange corner flags mark the High-fence parts: battery software, EV magnets and electrical steel.

## 2. Where the United States should secure, compete and stay open

### High fence — what cannot fail

Secure is a narrow call for the United States: one whole step and four layers inside other steps, which group into the three capabilities below. Secure does not mean making everything at home. It means being able to survive a prolonged cutoff or a hostile command, through qualified trusted supply, stocks, redundancy or control of the command path. [3]

| Capability | Why it matters | Main vulnerability | Recommended response |
|---|---|---|---|
| The rare-earth chain: separation, metals and magnets | Feeds the traction motors of electric vehicles. The United States holds 18% of world exports in the refining step's trade but only 1% of world supply, so the export share is a trade position to build from, not proven separation capacity. | China holds 94% of world refining supply and trusted suppliers 4%; substitution is hard and rebuilding takes more than three years. | **Mixed.** Build separation and metal-making at home where a foothold exists; add trusted refining and magnet capacity with allies through co-investment and offtake; hold stocks until trusted supply qualifies, and phase in adversary-source restrictions as it does. |
| Grain-oriented electrical steel | The core steel of power and distribution transformers, on which every grid expansion depends; it sits inside grid metals refining, a step otherwise left open. | Essential, import-dependent and slow to rebuild, in a step whose supply-chain need is 85; the need is measured for grid metals refining as a whole, not for the steel alone. | **Resilience, not replacement.** Stocks and spares pools sized to the rebuild time, long-lead procurement frameworks and capacity-reservation agreements with trusted producers; screening of foreign control of essential supply assets. |
| Control software of battery systems | Battery-management firmware, energy-management software, gateways and cloud fleet management can command, update or disable storage from abroad. | China supplies 56% of US imports of battery cells and packs, and the control is built partly into the hardware. | **Trusted control.** Trusted-source and certification rules for firmware, communications and remote access; limits on remote access and over-the-air updates by untrusted vendors; a deadline to replace untrusted control in the installed base. Where control is built into the hardware, trusted-source rules for that hardware and support for redesigns that separate the control layer. |

Sources: 2, 3, 4.

### Medium fence — where industrial capability matters

**Preserve.** The United States holds a top-quarter position in 13 high-value producing steps, across batteries and vehicles, reactors and nuclear fuel, transformers, wind and solar equipment, and gas and oil equipment. For most, need is low and the task is to stay at the frontier. In battery cells and packs, where need is still high (72), keeping the position means expanding it, with support tied to benchmarks and the gate open to allied investment. Reactors and steam generators (90), nuclear fuel and reactor parts (84) and transformers, switchgear and cables (92) clear the top quarter on moderate evidence; large transformers read Preserve with their step. The AIB's separate measure of reactor parts, 29% of world exports or production, corroborates the reactor-parts side of both nuclear steps; it does not test fuel fabrication. Gas turbines and LNG plant and drilling rigs, tankers and oil equipment sit above the line (80 and 81). The magnets of blades, towers and nacelle parts read Preserve on that step's measures but Secure in EV drivetrains; this brief treats them as part of the rare-earth chain. [1, 2, 3]

**Catch Up.** Battery materials refining is the best-anchored case for building: high value, a low position and five capability anchors, from operating capacity to committed investment. China holds 94% of world supply, so trusted capacity here is scarce for every ally. [2, 3]

The other Catch Up calls rest on the weakest anchors, and this brief does not build on them. Uranium conversion and enrichment rests on a small share of world exports, about 1%, and on the position in fuel and reactor parts next to it; no conversion or enrichment capacity is measured. Battery recycling rests only on a share of world exports (12%) of what the AIB analysis calls battery recycling feed. [2, 3]

**Partner.** Battery mineral mining and EV mineral mining have high need and no top-quarter position, and allied supply covers the need: source them from Australia and Canada. In rare-earth and wind materials refining, allied supply falls short, and the recommended partners are Germany, Belgium and Spain. Most of that step's trade by value is copper wire rod, not rare earths; its rare-earth separation layer reads Catch Up and belongs with the Secure chain, so partnering applies to the wider basket. [1, 2, 3]

### Low fence — what not to prioritize

Watch is not a failure: it means affordable, diversified access matters more than domestic production. Of the 25 Watch steps, 9 are deployment steps below the Accelerate bar. Most others are large, commoditized yards in mining and basic processing, left open even where need is high. [3]

- **Gas liquids processing and oil refining.** The United States holds 38% and 16% of world supply, the largest allied positions; in gas liquids it is a single point of failure for allies. Keep them open: the obligation is reliable supply to allies, not a domestic fence. [1, 2, 3]
- **Uranium, copper and bauxite, and PV mineral mining.** Need is high (99, 91 and 88), but these are large, commoditized yards. The configured answer is diversified trusted imports and at most a limited, stated capability floor. [2, 3]
- **Drilling and pump components, coal-plant components and grid components.** Supply-chain need and value are low, even where the position is in the top quarter: buy competitively.

Wafers and PV cells and polysilicon refining are not Low-fence cases: they read Watch as whole steps, but their cell, wafer and polysilicon layers read Preserve and Catch Up. [3]

## 3. The United States' allied bargain

**What the United States contributes.** The Allied Industrial Base analysis protects the demand of a trusted network of 64 economies; the United States is a core member of the trusted network. In that analysis it is a Demand & Market Anchor with resource & processing and capital & investment roles. It ranks first among allies on these measures: share of allied demand, net imports of end-use goods, resource & processing positions, share of world resource & processing supply, outward direct investment. It is the best-placed trusted partner of 119 economies worldwide somewhere in their supply chains, 49 of them for battery cells and packs. It holds 15% of world exports or production of semiconductor manufacturing equipment, 15% of automatic controllers and 12% of hydrogen, ammonia and methanol. In AC generators it holds 24%, yet allies need new capacity there, and the United States is the first-placed host for it. It is the largest allied holder in gas liquids processing (43%, with the United Arab Emirates and Canada next) and in gas-turbine and drilling components (20%). It is also the largest allied holder in drilling rigs, tankers and oil equipment (27%) and in battery cells and packs (6% of world supply). [1, 2, 3]

**What the United States needs.** If China's supply stopped, as in the Decoupling scenario, the United States would lose US$20.4 billion a year of imports across the Allied Industrial Base basket. New capacity at the best-placed hosts, led by Singapore, would restore about US$9.8 billion of it a year. [1, 2]

**The dependencies to make intentional.** Four bargains turn the American market and capital into trusted capacity that allies and the United States can count on.

## 4. Development strategy — build trusted capacity by helping partners Catch Up

Development cooperation can be the development arm of that bargain. A partnership needs evidence on both sides. On the US side, a step needs more trusted capacity: it is Partner, or Secure with allied delivery, its need is high, allied supply falls short, or allies need new capacity there. On the partner's side, a developing economy's same step is a credible Catch Up, with a capability anchor corroborated by more than context signals, inside the AIB trust screen. 47 pairs qualify, with 16 developing partners across 11 steps. [1, 3, 5]

The pairs that serve the US supply chain itself come first. 7 pairs, with 6 partners, sit in two steps where US need is high or allied supply falls short: rare-earth and wind materials refining and battery cells and packs. Another 40 rest on the allied gap alone, a reason shared by every donor in scope. [1, 3, 5]

The table takes the leading partner of the highest-ranked steps, each partner once. Mexico, an upper-middle-income economy with a Catch Up anchor in an own-need step, suits finance, guarantees and offtake. India (lower middle income) needs groundwork first: project preparation, infrastructure, permitting and skills. Its anchor is an export share without a measurable position. Madagascar, Viet Nam and Indonesia rest on the allied gap alone. [1, 3, 5]

## 5. Energy-system priorities

Only battery storage and gas networks and use clear the Accelerate bar of 60. Storage rests on all three measures of deployment need with high confidence, and grew from 3.2 to 56.6 GW between 2020 and 2025. Gas networks clear the bar narrowly, on two measures with moderate confidence, led by demand adequacy. [3, 11]

Nuclear power and grid networks sit just below the bar, nuclear on demand adequacy (82) and the grid on system need (79). Solar and wind read lower: demand adequacy is high for both, but system need is low (18). [3, 11, 12]

## 6. Is current policy pointed in the right direction?

Policy never decides a choice here; it is compared with it afterwards, using the United States' own recorded industrial-policy measures. Protection is a rank within the country, not a level.

|---|---|---|---|

Government should change course in wind turbines and in solar wafers and polysilicon. First, the call on wind turbines is Preserve, but recorded policy priority is low (16 within the country). It needs the R&D, workforce and finance for expansion a Preserve call sets. Second, protection sits in the top third of the country's steps for wafers and PV cells (68) and polysilicon refining (96) as whole steps, while only their cell, wafer and polysilicon layers read Preserve and Catch Up. Protection should narrow to those layers, calibrated and temporary. [3, 13, 14]

The flag on rare-earth and wind materials refining is recorded at the step, so the data cannot tell whether it targets rare-earth separation, which this brief supports, or the wider basket. Nuclear power reads Watch by a fraction, and its local-content measures overlap with the reactor Preserve call. The gap in battery recycling rests on an export anchor that does not measure recycling; the row is a data flag, not a call for new support. [3]

## 7. Strategic agenda — what should the United States do next?

### 1. Secure the rare-earth chain through a mixed domestic and allied build-out

Build separation and metal-making at home where a foothold exists or can be built; the step's export share, 18%, is the only measured foothold, against 1% of world supply. Add trusted refining and magnet capacity with allies through co-investment and offtake, hold stocks until it qualifies, and phase in adversary-source restrictions as it does. The allied route runs through refiners such as Japan and Australia and makers such as Germany, Japan and Mexico, and through new capacity in Malaysia and Thailand on commercial terms. [1, 2, 3, 5]

### 2. Put electrical steel and large transformers on a resilience footing

Hold stocks and spares of grain-oriented electrical steel sized to the rebuild time, set long-lead procurement frameworks, and sign capacity-reservation agreements with trusted producers. Keep the large-transformer base competitive at home through finance for expansion and workforce and supplier development, tied to benchmarks, with the gate open to allied investment from makers such as Germany and India. [1]

### 3. Keep battery manufacturing at home and fence its control software

Expand cell and pack production with time-limited support tied to milestones, open to allied and conditional investment. Require certified, trusted firmware and communications for battery systems, and replace untrusted control in the installed base on a deadline. In battery materials refining, tie time-limited incentives to benchmarks and welcome joint ventures that transfer capability.

### 4. Keep reactors and nuclear fuel competitive and source enrichment from allies

Keep the reactor, fuel and reactor-parts base at the frontier through process and next-generation R&D, workforce and supplier development, and finance for expansion, tied to benchmarks. Source conversion and enrichment from trusted processors through co-investment and offtake; the compact's raw-material and processing seats, Australia and India, are model seats, not measured enrichment supply. A domestic enrichment build is outside this call: it rests on a small export share with no capacity measured, so it waits until domestic capacity shows in the data. [1]

### 5. Build trusted cell and rare-earth capacity with developing partners

Start with Mexico in battery cells and packs, an own-need step of the partnership data where a US factory project already sits. Lead with development finance, guarantees and offtake, and use project preparation and skills where the constraint is a public good. Prepare India in rare-earth and wind materials refining over a longer horizon. The objective is both an additional trusted supplier and a durable manufacturing capability in the partner economy. This supplements the home build rather than replacing it. The United States Preserves cells and packs with domestic delivery, and the policy design for Preserve sets no partner-country finance, so the case rests on the own-need reasons the partnership data record, not on a Preserve mechanism. [1, 3, 5]

### 6. Accelerate storage and gas networks, back wind turbines, and keep commodity yards open

Deploy storage and gas networks faster through infrastructure, permitting and procurement, with trusted-supplier and security conditions on public support. Give the wind-turbine base the build a Preserve call needs. Keep the Watch mining steps, fuels processing and grid components open. Keep supply reliable in gas liquids, which allies rely on most, and in refined fuels, which they rely on less.

## Appendix — evidence and method

**How this brief is built.** Every number comes from the Country page data of the Allied Industrial Base Explorer (the data release of 26 November 2026) and from the Allied Industrial Base analysis. Each technology is split into six supply-chain stages, and each step gets a strategic choice by fixed rules applied in order: [3]

- Secure where an acute vulnerability is measured;
- at deployment, Accelerate where deployment need reaches 60; [3]
- Watch where the step is a large, commoditized yard;
- Preserve where Competitive Position is in the top quarter (75) and either Strategic Industrial Value (bar 60) or supply-chain need (bar 67) is high; [3]
- Catch Up where the value is high, the position is not, and a capability anchor is corroborated by other evidence;
- Partner where supply-chain need is high but there is neither a top-quarter position nor, for a high-value step, a credible pathway;
- Watch otherwise.

Named layers inside a step, such as control software or magnets, take their own choice, and a step whose parts sit behind different fences is mixed. A value that rests on market size alone is capped just under the bar. Observed policy never decides a choice. The high fence (Secure) protects what cannot be allowed to fail; the medium fence covers capabilities worth building, keeping or sourcing deliberately and deployment to accelerate; the low fence (Watch) leaves a step open to competitive purchase.

**Review cases.** Some calls rest on low confidence, an engine review flag, a thin capability anchor, a known data artifact or, at deployment, a single indicator of low confidence. They stay on the map but never enter the Bottom line, the Agenda, the policy table or a headline mark. The brief treats three steps as review cases. [3]

**Evidence and caveats.** The rare-earth chokepoint rests on measured supply concentration, and the battery-materials Catch Up has high confidence. The Secure refining step and battery materials refining show the same 94% share of world supply, which suggests overlapping trade codes. Nuclear power sits just under the Accelerate bar. The uranium conversion and battery recycling Catch Ups rest on export shares that do not measure capacity, and the AIB's 44% share in battery recycling measures recycling feed. The capacity anchors of rare-earth and battery-metal refining come from battery-metal facility data, so the rare-earth foothold is the export share. The nuclear fuel, parts and reactor baskets mix nuclear lines with generic boiler and steam-turbine parts, so their top-quarter positions rest partly on generic parts; the AIB's reactor-parts measure is the cleaner signal. The transformer Preserve call rests on shares that also cover switchgear and cables, on moderate evidence and on a value just above the bar. The AIB analysis counts the United States as the only allied holder of electrolyser systems, at 19% of world supply, but the national map cannot assess hydrogen manufacturing, so this brief builds on neither reading. In Figure 3, the shares printed for the demand-anchor seats measure imports, not supply. 18 steps lack the data for a call, including geothermal supply-chain steps other than power and heat, green hydrogen manufacturing, wind mineral mining and most recycling. Competitive Position ranks the United States among economies that already supply a step, so a zero means no measurable position. Partner lists name where allied capacity sits, which can include trading hubs, not who owns it. The AIB status 'allied supply covers the need' for rare-earth and battery-metal refining describes the allied model, not ample trusted supply: the Secure call rests on a 4% trusted share of world supply. [1, 2, 3]

**Development partnerships.** A pair joins a US step with a strategic reason for more allied capacity and a developing economy (not high income) whose same step is a Catch Up. The partner needs an anchor corroborated beyond context signals, sufficient evidence and confidence, and a place inside the AIB trust screen; on a step whose data describe different products, the anchor must measure the product the need is about. A partner that already hosts the step's new capacity in the AIB, or Preserves it, is a commercial case. Own-need pairs are those where the US step itself needs more trusted capacity; the others rest on the allied gap alone. Aid comes from the OECD Creditor Reporting System by purpose code. Components and Assembly share codes, no code funds recycling, and many codes name a stage rather than a technology, so aid at a step is not aid for that technology. A constraint on a partner is named only where the data observe its input; otherwise the table says the data do not cover it. In the relationship table an observed zero is stated as nothing recorded, data that do not cover a pair are left out, and the direct investment position is economy-wide, not tied to the step. Export-credit finance is reported apart and never counted as aid. Development Potential, an older partner score, is a supporting indicator only.
