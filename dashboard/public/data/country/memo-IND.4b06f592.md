# India and the Allied Industrial Base

*What to secure, build, source from partners, and deploy*

*Generated from Allied Industrial Base Explorer data; evidence, sources and caveats in the appendix. Data as of 26 November 2026.*

## 1. Bottom line — India's strategic role

India is a partner in the trusted network behind the Allied Industrial Base, not a core member, yet it already holds a part in all of it. It holds a role in all ten strategic compacts and core seats in Solar Manufacturing, Wind & Hydropower Equipment, Nuclear Fuel & Reactor Systems, Grid Equipment & Conductors and Industrial Electrification & Hydrogen. Its distinctive contribution is clean-energy equipment, where it ranks in the top quarter of suppliers although its share of world supply is small (2% in wind turbines, 2% in PV modules and inverters). Allies rely on it for 11% of world exports or production of wind blades and hubs and 13% of overhead conductors, and it holds top-quarter positions in wind turbines, PV modules and EV drivetrains that it should preserve. Its main industrial build is batteries, where cells, packs and components rest on committed investment and China supplies 92% of cell and pack imports. Development partners with their own need for trusted battery supply can help that build reach scale. The fence that matters is high but narrow: the rare-earth chain, and the control software of the grid, inverters and batteries India installs. The larger task is deployment: storage, solar, wind, grid and nuclear power all need to be built faster. [1, 2, 3]

- **Secure.** The rare-earth chain, where China holds 94% of world refining supply and trusted suppliers only 5%; the control software of grid equipment, inverters and batteries; reactor control; and remote access to turbines and vehicles. The answer is qualified trusted supply and control of command paths, not self-sufficiency. [3]
- **Build / preserve.** Preserve wind turbines and their parts, where allies already rely on India's blades and hubs, and PV modules, EV drivetrains and vehicles, where the AIB shows little or no supplier role. Catch Up in battery cells, packs and components, the cases with high need and value, anchored on committed investment rather than on today's output, and in rare-earth separation as part of the Secure chain.
- **Partner.** Battery and EV minerals, from producers India's own trust screen accepts, such as Australia, Chile and Zimbabwe. Their supply already covers India's need, so the task is terms and diversification. [1, 2, 3]
- **Deploy / stay open.** Accelerate battery storage, solar and wind power, grid networks and nuclear power; coal power and gas networks also clear the bar. Buy commodity hardware competitively, with security conditions on equipment that carries built-in control. Leave open uranium conversion and enrichment, gas turbines and LNG plant, grid metals refining, and coal, copper and bauxite mining.

In Figure 1 the color sits in the manufacturing columns and in deployment, which is almost all Accelerate. India holds Preserve positions in wind, solar, EV and coal-plant equipment, and Catch Up runs down the battery row and through nuclear manufacturing. The first columns are mostly Watch or Partner: India buys its minerals and most refined inputs. The orange corner flags mark control software in battery, grid, solar, wind, vehicle, reactor and oil equipment, and the magnets in EV drivetrains and wind parts.

## 2. Where India should secure, compete and stay open

### High fence — what cannot fail

Secure is a narrow call for India: one whole step and 12 layers inside other steps, which group into the five capabilities below. Secure means being able to survive a prolonged cutoff or a hostile command, through qualified trusted supply, stocks, redundancy or control of the command path. [1]

### Medium fence — where industrial capability matters

Each step turns on whether the capability is worth having (Strategic Industrial Value) and how strong India is today (Competitive Position).

**Preserve.** Wind turbines and blades, towers and nacelle parts hold top-quarter positions, and allies treat India as an essential supplier of blades and hubs. PV modules and inverters also sit in the top quarter, with operating capacity and committed investment behind them. EV drivetrain components are strong; EV and charger manufacturing sits on the top-quarter line, and in the Electric Vehicles compact India holds only demand and bridge roles, so it serves the home market more than allies. Coal-plant equipment and coal-plant components also read Preserve. The other Preserve calls are review cases listed in the appendix: grid components; transformers, switchgear and cables; drilling rigs, tankers and oil equipment; and polysilicon refining. [1, 2]

**Catch Up.** Battery cells and packs is the clearest case for building: high value and a position of 79, above the top-quarter line. But with <1% of world supply India is not yet a contender, so the call is Catch Up on committed investment, not today's output. Its committed investment is corroborated by feasibility, specialization in the adjacent step, industrial learning and announced projects, with high confidence. Battery components rest on the same kind of anchor, with less recorded policy behind them. Both are mixed in delivery: build at home where the base is established and with allied partners for the rest. Rare-earth and wind materials refining is the Catch Up part of the Secure rare-earth chain. Several other Catch Up calls should not carry the strategy. Reactors and steam generators (64) and nuclear fuel and reactor parts (43) rest on a trade basket that mixes reactor lines with generic boiler parts, so the calls are not evidence of a reactor industry. Battery materials refining rests on 1% of world exports with no plant in the capacity data, and battery recycling on no measured position. [1, 3]

**Partner.** India's Partner steps sit at the start of the chain. It holds no measurable position in battery mineral mining and only a weak one in EV mineral mining (24 out of a hundred). It depends heavily on imports, so supply should be spread across producers India's own trust screen accepts, such as Australia, Chile and Zimbabwe. Wafers and PV cells also read Partner, but the call is flagged for review by the engine. India holds a top-quarter position there and both named layers read Catch Up, a disagreement this brief does not settle. [1, 2, 3]

**Shared capabilities.** Some capabilities sit in several technology chains with different calls; the table keeps the national reading apart from what allies rely on India for. Overhead conductors, outside the table, show the gap: the cables layer of India's grid equipment reads Watch, while India holds 13% of world exports or production and a core seat that allies cannot easily replace. That is an allied role to keep reliable, not a domestic fence. [2, 3]

### Low fence — what not to prioritize

Watch is not a failure: it means affordable, diversified access matters more than domestic production.

- **Uranium conversion and enrichment.** Nuclear power must grow, but these steps have low supply-chain need and no capability anchor, and trusted suppliers hold 74% of world supply. Keep access diversified. [3]
- **Gas turbines, LNG plant and their components.** Gas networks read Accelerate, but the equipment behind them has low supply-chain need and is best bought competitively.
- **Grid metals refining.** Copper and aluminium processing is a commodity step with a capability floor.
- **Coal, copper and bauxite mining.** These are commodity steps: coal is best diversified toward trusted producers, and copper and bauxite need at most a specified capability floor.

## 3. India's allied bargain

**What India contributes.** The trusted network the Allied Industrial Base treats as allied has 64 economies, 28 of them partners like India. The analysis assigns India no primary strategic role at the current thresholds, yet it is a demand anchor and bridge in most compacts, with supplier seats that allies cannot easily replace in wind, nuclear, grid equipment and industrial electrification. It holds 11% of world exports or production of wind blades and hubs, 13% of overhead conductors and 16% of direct-reduced iron. Its nuclear seat rests on 41% of nuclear special materials, a trade line with no physical data. Under Managed Interdependence, the default scenario, India sits in the minimum coalition of 13 Sectoral Compacts, the AIB's finer workstreams within the strategic compacts. Under Decoupling it sits in 18, as a demand anchor in 16. [1, 2, 3]

**What India needs.** Minerals, refined inputs and battery capability. If China's supply stopped, as in the Decoupling scenario, India would lose US$36.3 billion a year of imports across the Allied Industrial Base basket. New capacity at the best-placed hosts, led by Singapore, would restore about US$19.0 billion of it a year. That counts new capacity only, not trade redirected to existing allied suppliers, so it is not a measure of how much of the loss allies could cover. The allies with the most supply restored to and from India by new and expanded trade are South Korea, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Singapore. The count runs in both directions, so it includes buyers of India's output as well as suppliers. [2, 3]

**The dependencies to make intentional.** These bargains turn that exchange into a specialization partners can count on.

## 4. Development partnerships — what support could help India Catch Up?

India is not high income, so this section asks what support could help it Catch Up and what developed partners would gain. A Catch Up step qualifies when its anchor is corroborated beyond context signals, its evidence and confidence suffice, and it measures the product partners need. Developed economies must also have their own need for more trusted supply there. That shows a shared interest, not capacity to give: some named partners do not report development aid to the OECD. Of India's seven Catch Up steps, four pass. [1, 2, 6]

The battery steps lead. Battery cells and packs and battery components rest on committed investment, and many developed economies share the need, among them Spain, the United Kingdom, Australia and Austria for cells and Oman, Finland, Australia and Belgium for components. Battery materials refining passes on a share of world exports alone (1%), and no plant appears in the capacity data, so feasibility studies and project preparation come first. It is a candidate for study, not a build commitment. Rare-earth and wind materials refining passes on India's exports of rare-earth compounds, a supply that the United Kingdom, South Korea, the United States and Austria need. In the table, 'high-need step' describes the partners' need, not India's. [1, 2, 3, 6]

The two nuclear steps (nuclear fuel and reactor parts; reactors and steam generators) do not pass, because their baskets mix reactor and fuel parts with generic boiler parts, and neither does battery recycling, for lack of evidence. [1]

India's Preserve steps in wind, solar and EV equipment are a commercial case, not aid: partners with their own need should trade with India, take offtake and invest on commercial terms.

## 5. Energy-system priorities

India needs almost every energy technology faster, regardless of where the equipment is made. Nuclear power, battery storage, solar and wind power and grid networks all clear the Accelerate bar of 60, each resting on every measure of deployment need with high confidence and led by demand adequacy. The national target of 560 GW of renewables by 2030 compares with 250 GW installed in 2025; at the recent build rate the shortfall is about 165 GW. Between 2020 and 2025, solar capacity grew from 39.7 to 135.5 GW and wind from 38.6 to 54.5 GW. The target and the shortfall cover renewables together, so the data do not say which technology must speed up most. [1, 5]

Coal power and gas networks also clear the bar, coal mainly on demand adequacy and gas on system need. Accelerate says the system needs more of what they provide; this analysis does not weigh them against clean sources. Recorded policy already ranks coal power among India's top deployment priorities. Geothermal power and heat reads Accelerate on fewer measures, with no geothermal supply step assessed, so it is a case to examine, not a priority.

High deployment priority does not imply high manufacturing priority: the configured build is infrastructure, permitting and procurement. Oil use also reads Accelerate, but on a single indicator of low confidence, use rising from 9.14 to 10.89 EJ; it is a review case, shown apart. As a supply-side question only: India's supply-chain need in crude oil is 78 out of a hundred, and the configured answer for that Watch step is supply diversified toward trusted producers. EV adoption and clean hydrogen sit below the bar: worth watching, not forcing. [1, 3, 8]

## 7. Strategic agenda — what should India do next?

### 1. Secure the rare-earth chain through a mixed domestic and allied build-out

Build separation and metal-making at home, starting from a small export foothold (about 2% of world exports, with no measurable share of world supply) and testing it with feasibility work before committing capacity. Build the step to magnets with allied makers such as Germany, Japan, the United States and Mexico through co-investment and offtake. Hold stocks sized to the time it takes to restore supply until trusted supply qualifies. Recorded policy barely reaches this step today, so it is the first gap to close. [2, 3]

### 2. Catch up in battery cells and components with time-limited support tied to benchmarks

Tie production incentives, first-of-a-kind finance, workforce and supplier development and demand creation to benchmarks, and let support sunset if the gap does not narrow. Keep the gate open to joint ventures on terms that transfer capability. Bring support for components up to the level of cells.

### 3. Keep the wind, solar and EV equipment base competitive as India's allied contribution

Wind turbines, blades, PV modules and EV drivetrains are positions to hold, not to rebuild. Back them with R&D, workforce and supplier development and finance for expansion tied to benchmarks, and welcome allied investment in new blade capacity (in the AIB, 1st host: wind blades & hubs). [1, 2]

### 4. Put a high, narrow fence around control software

Set trusted-source and certification rules for battery management, inverter firmware, smart meters, grid control, turbine controllers, connected vehicles and reactor control. Restrict remote access and over-the-air updates by untrusted vendors, and replace untrusted control software in the installed base. Whether India's own equipment makers can supply the certified alternative is not measured here and should be tested first.

### 5. Use development partnerships to bring battery cells and components to scale

Start with partners that share the need, such as Spain, the United Kingdom, Australia and Austria for battery cells and packs and Oman, Finland, Australia and Belgium for battery components. Use project preparation, supplier development, skills and certification first, then development finance, guarantees, offtake and joint ventures as plants come on line. The objective is a trusted second source for partners and a durable industry at home. [1, 2, 6]

### 6. Accelerate storage, solar, wind, grid and nuclear power while keeping sourcing open

Deploy faster through infrastructure, permitting, interoperability and procurement; buy commodity hardware competitively from trusted suppliers, with trusted-supplier and security conditions on public deployment support and connected equipment. Gas turbines, conversion and enrichment, and coal and copper mining are not localization priorities: affordable, diversified access is.

## Appendix — evidence and method

**How this brief is built.** Every number comes from the Country page data of the Allied Industrial Base Explorer (the data release of 26 November 2026) and from the Allied Industrial Base analysis. Each technology is split into supply-chain stages, from mining to recovery, and each step gets a strategic choice by fixed rules applied in order: [1]

- Secure where an acute vulnerability is measured;
- at deployment, Accelerate where deployment need reaches 60; [1]
- Watch where the step is a large, commoditized yard;
- Preserve where Competitive Position is in the top quarter (75) and either Strategic Industrial Value (bar 60) or supply-chain need (bar 67) is high; [1]
- Catch Up where the value is high, the position is not, and a capability anchor is corroborated by other evidence;
- Partner where supply-chain need is high but there is neither a top-quarter position nor, for a high-value step, a credible pathway;
- Watch otherwise.

Named layers inside a step, such as control software or magnets, take their own choice, and a step whose parts sit behind different fences is mixed. A value that rests on market size alone is capped just under the bar. Observed policy never decides a choice. The high fence (Secure) protects what cannot be allowed to fail; the medium fence covers capabilities worth building, keeping or sourcing deliberately and deployment to accelerate; the low fence (Watch) leaves a step open to competitive purchase.

**Review cases.** Some calls rest on low confidence, an engine review flag, a thin capability anchor, a known data artifact or, at deployment, a single indicator of low confidence. They stay on the map but never enter the Bottom line, the Agenda, the policy table or a headline mark. 8 steps are review cases. [1]

|---|---|---|

**Evidence and caveats.** India is a partner in the trusted network, not a core member; the brief applies the same method as for core members. Both nuclear manufacturing steps rest on trade baskets that mix reactor and fuel lines with generic boiler and steam-turbine parts, which the shares cannot tell apart. Reactors and nuclear fuel parts are therefore not treated as established or emerging industries. India's partner lists come from its own trust screen, which admits some economies outside the trusted network, such as Myanmar and Zimbabwe. The AIB nuclear seat rests on a trade line in nuclear special materials with no physical data. The AIB flags its wind-blade line as usable with caution: its trade headings are broad, and production is more concentrated than trade.
