# Japan and the Allied Industrial Base

*What to secure, build, source from partners, and deploy*

*Generated from Allied Industrial Base Explorer data; evidence, sources and caveats in the appendix. Data as of 26 November 2026.*

## 1. Bottom line — Japan's strategic role

Japan's distinctive role in the Allied Industrial Base is as its specialist supplier and manufacturer: it holds a role in nine of the ten strategic compacts, and 63 economies worldwide count it as their best-placed trusted partner somewhere in the chain. Its exposure sits at the start of the chain: imported fuel, minerals it does not mine, and refined rare earths. It should keep its top-quarter positions in battery cells and packs and in reactors. Its industrial effort should go into battery materials refining and battery components, where its anchors are among the strongest and allies need new capacity, and into building on its position in wind components. For minerals and wind materials it should depend deliberately on trusted producers, and its development cooperation can help developing partners add the battery and wind capacity allies lack. At home, deployment is as pressing: nuclear, solar and wind power need to be built faster. The high fence belongs around the rare-earth chain, fuel supply, and the control software that can command Japan's grid, batteries, inverters, turbines and vehicles. [1, 2]

- **Secure.** The rare-earth chain from separation to magnets, where China holds 94% of world supply of rare-earth and battery-metal refining, which covers battery metals as well as rare earths, and trusted suppliers only 4%. The supply of oil, gas and coal, which takes one to three years to rebuild. The control software of grid, battery, inverter, turbine and vehicle equipment. The answer is trusted supply, stocks and control of command paths, not self-sufficiency. [2, 3, 4]
- **Build / preserve.** Preserve the top-quarter positions in battery cells and packs and in reactors and steam generators. Catch Up in battery materials refining and battery components, among the best-anchored cases and with high need, where allies also need new capacity, and build on the position in wind components. Keep specialist supply, from chip equipment to transformer steel, reliable for allies: an allied commitment, not a domestic fence.
- **Partner.** Battery and EV minerals from trusted producers such as Australia, the United States and Canada, and wind materials, where allied supply falls short. With developing partners, add trusted battery and wind capacity through development finance and offtake. [1, 2, 3]
- **Deploy / stay open.** Accelerate nuclear, solar and wind power and gas networks, buying commodity hardware competitively from trusted suppliers, with security conditions on connected equipment. Leave open wafers and PV cells, polysilicon refining and gas-plant equipment.

In Figure 1, Secure and Partner in the first column mark the fuels and minerals Japan must import. The middle columns hold its Preserve and most of its Catch Up cases, and deployment its Accelerate cases. The orange corner flags mark High-fence parts inside other steps, from control software and reactor controls to rare-earth magnets and separation. Violet flags mark Catch Up layers inside Watch steps, such as solar-grade polysilicon and grain-oriented electrical steel; they inherit their steps' anchors.

### Medium fence — where industrial capability matters

This is where Japan makes its real industrial choices, by asking whether a capability is worth having (Strategic Industrial Value) and how strong Japan is today (Competitive Position). For Japan, Catch Up in components and assembly often means closing a gap to the frontier from a substantial base; in battery materials refining it means building from a low position.

**Preserve.** Battery cells and packs (76) and reactors and steam generators (83) clear the top-quarter bar (75) with moderate confidence. In cells and packs need is high (76), so keeping the position means expanding it, with finance tied to benchmarks and the gate open to allied investment. EV drivetrain components, EV and charger manufacturing and nuclear fuel and reactor parts also read Preserve, near the bar, but on low confidence, and coal-plant components reads Partner on low confidence. [2, 3]

**Catch Up.** Battery materials refining is the clearest case for building: high value, a low position (19), three capability anchors and high confidence. China holds 94% of world supply and trusted suppliers 4%, so trusted capacity is scarce for every ally. Battery components is also well anchored: Japan holds 13% of world exports and has operating capacity behind them, and 4 economies have it as their best-placed trusted partner. Yet 77% of its own imports come from China. Blades, towers and nacelle parts builds on a mid-table position (48) with high confidence; its magnets sit behind the High fence. [2, 3]

Other Catch Up calls are weaker than they look, and this brief does not build on them:

- **Wind turbines:** a position of 44 on a world supply share of 2%, in a step whose trade codes also cover cables, substations and transformers. [2, 3]
- **PV modules and inverters:** moderate confidence, conventional modules read Watch, and the trade codes include machines for making wafers and chips. [2]
- **Uranium conversion and enrichment:** an inherited anchor (a strong position in the next step up the chain) and a world supply share of <1%, so trusted allied enrichment comes first. [2, 3]
- **Battery recycling:** a single export-share anchor in a step whose trade codes are battery waste, scrap and residues, with no position measure.

**Partner.** Japan holds 0% of world supply in battery and EV minerals and the data show no credible pathway to scale it. So dependence should be deliberate and spread across trusted producers such as Australia, the United States and Canada; allied supply covers the need. In rare-earth and wind materials refining, allied supply sits in Germany, Belgium and Spain and falls short, so co-investment and offtake must add capacity. Its rare-earth separation layer belongs to the High fence. [1, 2, 3]

### Low fence — what not to prioritize

A credible strategy also says what not to localize. Watch is not a failure: affordable, diversified access matters more than domestic production, and for Japan it often marks positions that compete without help.

- **Wafers and PV cells, and polysilicon refining.** Supply-chain need for wafers and cells is low (32), and polysilicon refining is a large, commoditized yard worth at most a stated capability floor. Wafers and cells rank among Japan's most protected steps in its own measures, but the trade codes include chip wafers and chip-machine parts, so some of that may be chip policy. [2, 3]
- **Gas turbines, LNG plant and their components.** Japan holds top-quarter positions (96 and 98) on modest need and low value; its makers compete for the gas build-out without a fence. [2, 3]
- **Grid metals refining and grid components.** Need is low in components (36) but high in grid metals refining (75), a large, commoditized yard; the position in components is in the top quarter (85). Grain-oriented electrical steel reads Catch Up only by inheriting its step's position, while allies rely on Japan for transformer steel: a supplier role to keep reliable. [2, 3]

## 3. Japan's allied bargain

**What Japan contributes.** In the Allied Industrial Base analysis, which protects the demand of a trusted network of 64 economies, Japan is a core member of the trusted network. It combines capital & investment, specialized technology and scale manufacturing roles, and it ranks first among allies on its measure of strength (compacts with a specialist position). It supplies 20% of world exports or production of semiconductor manufacturing equipment, 25% of wafers and 11% of automatic controllers. It holds a core seat in five compacts: Permanent Magnets & Drive Systems, Battery Materials & Cells, Solar Manufacturing, Electric Vehicles and Heat Pumps & Cooling. It is the largest allied holder of wafers and PV cells (11%) and PV modules and inverters (9%), but those positions rest partly on chip equipment and wafers that share the steps' trade codes, so they overstate its PV output. [1, 2, 3]

**What Japan needs.** Minerals, refined rare earths, wind materials and fuel. If China's supply stopped, as in the Decoupling scenario, Japan would lose US$19.9 billion a year of imports across the Allied Industrial Base basket. New capacity at the best-placed hosts, led by Singapore, would restore only about US$6.3 billion of it a year. Under Managed Interdependence, the default scenario, Japan sits in the minimum coalition of 9 Sectoral Compacts (the finer units beneath the strategic compacts); under Decoupling, 20, in 16 of them as a demand anchor. [1, 3]

**The dependencies to make intentional.** These bargains turn that exchange into a specialization partners can count on.

## 4. Development strategy — build trusted capacity by helping partners Catch Up

Development cooperation can be the development arm of that bargain, and a partnership needs evidence on both sides. On the Japanese side, a step needs more trusted capacity. On the partner's side, a developing economy's same step is a credible Catch Up with a corroborated anchor, inside the AIB trust screen. 47 pairs qualify, with 16 developing partners across 11 steps. [1, 2, 5]

Japan's own supply chain supports 26 pairs with 12 partners across five steps, and the table leads with them: rare-earth and wind materials refining; battery materials refining; battery components; battery cells and packs; and wind turbines. The other 21 rest on the allied gap alone, a reason that is the same for every donor in scope. Viet Nam and the Philippines are lower middle income, so project preparation, infrastructure and skills come first. Indonesia, Mexico and Malaysia are upper-middle-income economies, where finance, guarantees and offtake should lead. The Philippines has a Competitive Position of 0, as do the other partners in its step, so its case rests on a share of world exports and operating capacity. Malaysia's case rests on trade shares alone, with no plant in the capacity data and a position of 27. [1, 2, 3, 5, 6]

Battery materials refining is among the broadest strands, level with battery components: 7 developing economies, led by the Philippines and Madagascar, have credible pathways, and Indonesia, Argentina, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Malaysia already host new capacity commercially. For rare-earth and battery-metal refining, the Secure step of the rare-earth chain: No credible development-led supply solution identified. Thailand and Malaysia host its new capacity commercially, and no partner qualifies in minerals or fuels. Another 33 pairs are commercial, where trade, offtake and investment fit better than aid. [1, 2, 5]

## 5. Energy-system priorities

Wherever the equipment is made, nuclear, solar and wind power and gas networks clear the Accelerate bar of 60. Solar and wind rest on all three measures with high confidence, though system need drives them: demand adequacy alone (41 and 49) is below the bar. The renewables target of 201 GW by 2030 compares with 133 GW installed in 2025, a shortfall of about 47 GW at the recent build rate. Between 2020 and 2025, solar grew from 71.9 to 92.2 GW and wind from 4.1 to 6.2 GW; nuclear capacity was flat (33.1 to 33.1 GW). [2, 11, 12]

High deployment priority does not imply high manufacturing priority. Japan has Catch Up cases behind nuclear and wind, but deployment should not wait for them: equipment should come from whichever trusted supplier is ready. Gas reads Accelerate on system need, as a firming fuel whose supply sits behind the High fence. Clean hydrogen also reads Accelerate, but on a single indicator of low confidence, so it is a review case. Battery storage, grid networks and EV adoption sit below the bar: worth watching, not forcing.

## 7. Strategic agenda — what should Japan do next?

### 1. Secure the rare-earth chain with allied partners

Build trusted separation, metal-making and magnet capacity at home and with allies such as the United States and Australia through co-investment and offtake; hold stocks until it qualifies, and phase in limits on adversary sources only as it does. [1, 2, 3]

### 2. Hold fuel security through stocks and trusted contracts

Oil, gas and coal are Secure for resilience: they are essential and imported, and their supply takes one to three years to rebuild. Size strategic stocks to that time, reserve capacity with trusted producers such as Canada, the United States and Norway for oil (partner evidence is weak, so test the contract terms), and stress-test the loss of the largest supplier. [1, 2, 3, 4]

### 3. Catch up in battery materials and components with time-limited support tied to benchmarks

Tie production incentives, first-of-a-kind finance, workforce and supplier development and demand creation to benchmarks, with sunsets, and keep the gate open to licensing and joint ventures that transfer capability. Build battery components at home where the base is established and with allied partners for the rest.

### 4. Turn mineral dependence into investment bargains with trusted producers

Buy battery and EV minerals through offtake and co-investment with producers such as Australia, the United States and Canada, with export-credit finance for their capacity and contracts spread across several suppliers. [1, 2, 3]

### 5. Build trusted battery and wind capacity with developing partners

Start with the Philippines in battery materials refining and Indonesia in battery components. Test Malaysia in wind turbines first, where the data record no operating or committed plant and the step's trade codes also cover cables, substations and transformers. Prepare Viet Nam in rare-earth and wind materials refining over a longer horizon. Use project preparation, infrastructure and skills where the constraint is a public good, and development finance, guarantees and offtake where it is bankability. [1, 2, 5]

### 6. Accelerate nuclear, solar and wind while keeping sourcing open

Deploy faster through infrastructure, permitting and procurement; buy commodity hardware competitively from trusted suppliers, and attach trusted-supplier and security conditions to public deployment support and to equipment with built-in control.

## Appendix — evidence and method

**How this brief is built.** Every number comes from the Country page data of the Allied Industrial Base Explorer (the data release of 26 November 2026) and from the Allied Industrial Base analysis. Each technology is split into supply-chain stages from extraction to recycling, and each step gets a strategic choice by fixed rules applied in order: [2]

- Secure where an acute vulnerability is measured;
- at deployment, Accelerate where deployment need reaches 60; [2]
- Watch where the step is a large, commoditized yard;
- Preserve where Competitive Position is in the top quarter (75) and either Strategic Industrial Value (bar 60) or supply-chain need (bar 67) is high; [2]
- Catch Up where the value is high, the position is not, and a capability anchor is corroborated by other evidence;
- Partner where supply-chain need is high but there is neither a top-quarter position nor, for a high-value step, a credible pathway;
- Watch otherwise.

Named layers inside a step, such as control software or magnets, take their own choice, and a step whose parts sit behind different fences is mixed. A value that rests on market size alone is capped just under the bar. Observed policy never decides a choice. The high fence (Secure) protects what cannot be allowed to fail; the medium fence covers capabilities worth building, keeping or sourcing deliberately and deployment to accelerate; the low fence (Watch) leaves a step open to competitive purchase.

**Evidence and caveats.** The rare-earth chokepoint rests on measured supply concentration, and the battery-materials Catch Up has high confidence. Japan's solar positions, in the choices and in the AIB, rest partly on trade codes it shares with the chip industry: silicon wafers, parts of chip-making machines and machines for making wafers and chips. They overstate its position in PV cells and modules. The wind turbines step's trade codes also cover cables, substations and transformers, and battery recycling's are battery waste, scrap and metal residues. Uranium conversion and enrichment rests on an adjacent-step anchor, with a world supply share of <1%. The Secure refining step and the Partner wind-materials step share the rare-earth trade codes. 18 steps lack the data for a call, including geothermal supply, green hydrogen manufacturing and most recycling. Recorded policy ranks electrolyser stacks (98) and systems (99) near the top of Japan's own measures, but those steps cannot be assessed and deployment need reads high on a single indicator, so this brief does not settle that disagreement. The review cases EV and charger manufacturing and transformers, switchgear and cables also read as needing more policy attention. Competitive Position ranks Japan among economies that already supply a step, so a zero means no measurable position. Partner lists name where allied capacity sits, which can include trading hubs, not who owns it. [2, 3, 13, 14]

**Development partnerships.** A pair joins a Japanese step with a strategic reason for more allied capacity and a developing economy (not high income) whose same step is a Catch Up. The reason is Japan's own need, or the allied gap alone, where allies need new capacity, which is the same for every donor in scope. The partner needs an anchor corroborated beyond context signals, sufficient evidence and confidence, and a place inside the AIB trust screen; on a step whose data describe different products, the anchor must measure the product the need is about. A partner that already hosts the step's new capacity in the AIB, or Preserves it, is a commercial case. Aid comes from the OECD Creditor Reporting System by purpose code and covers energy, industry and mining aid only. Components and Assembly share codes, no code funds recycling, and many codes name a stage rather than a technology, so aid at a step is not aid for that technology. Aid is called technology-coded only where codes naming the technology hold at least half of it. A constraint on a partner is named only where the data observe its input; otherwise the table says the data do not cover it. In the relationship table an observed zero is stated as nothing recorded, data that do not cover a pair are left out, and the direct investment position is economy-wide, not tied to the step. Export-credit finance is reported apart and never counted as aid, and direct investment uses the IMF positions (the partner's reports where Japan reports none).
