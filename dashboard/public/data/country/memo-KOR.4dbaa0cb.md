# South Korea and the Allied Industrial Base

*What to secure, build, source from partners, and deploy*

*Generated from Allied Industrial Base Explorer data; evidence, sources and caveats in the appendix. Data as of 26 November 2026.*

## 1. Bottom line — South Korea's strategic role

South Korea's distinctive role in the Allied Industrial Base is as its battery maker and equipment builder. It holds 36% of world exports or production of cathode materials and precursors. It is the largest allied holder of battery components and of gas turbines and LNG plant, and it is the best-placed trusted partner of 65 economies worldwide for at least one step. Nationally, that strength reads Preserve in battery cells and components, EV drivetrains and vehicles, reactors, PV modules and transformers. In battery materials and gas plant it reads only Catch Up: South Korea is a leading cathode supplier and the largest allied holder of gas plant, but its national positions sit below the top quarter of producers. Its exposure lies at the start of the chain. It is among the most import-dependent economies for oil, gas and coal, relies on China for refined rare earths, and buys 44% of its imported uranium conversion and enrichment from Russia. For minerals and rare-earth and wind materials it should depend deliberately on trusted producers. At home, the energy system needs nuclear, solar and wind power and electric vehicles faster. Its development cooperation can follow its firms' investment abroad, helping partners add trusted battery and drivetrain capacity. [1, 2, 3]

- **Secure.** Imported oil, gas and coal; the rare-earth chain from separation to magnets, where China holds 94% of world refining supply and trusted suppliers only 4%; uranium conversion and enrichment; and the control software of grids, batteries and inverters. The answer is trusted contracts, stocks and control of command paths, not self-sufficiency. [2]
- **Build / preserve.** Preserve battery cells, packs and components, EV drivetrains and vehicles, reactors, PV modules and inverters, and transformers and switchgear. Catch Up in battery materials, where the national position trails the allied role, and in nuclear fuel parts and wind towers.
- **Partner.** Battery and EV minerals, rare-earth and wind materials, and drilling and pump components. Source them from trusted producers, such as the United States, Canada and Australia for minerals and Germany, Belgium and Spain for wind materials, and add trusted capacity through co-investment and offtake where allied supply falls short. [1, 2, 3]
- **Deploy / stay open.** Accelerate nuclear, solar and wind power and EV adoption. Leave open oil refining, gas liquids processing, polysilicon beyond its solar-grade layer, and uranium, copper and bauxite ores, where diversified access matters more than protection.

The orange corner flags mark High-fence parts inside steps it otherwise builds: control software in batteries, grid equipment, inverters, turbines, vehicles and plant, and rare-earth magnets in drivetrains and wind parts.

## 2. Where South Korea should secure, compete and stay open

### High fence — what cannot fail

Secure is a broad call for South Korea: five whole steps and 14 layers inside other steps, which group into ten capabilities. Secure means being able to survive a prolonged cutoff or a hostile command, through qualified trusted supply, stocks, redundancy or control of the command path. Remote access to turbines and connected vehicles, reactor control systems and oil and gas network controls are also Secure, with the same answer as grid software. Grain-oriented electrical steel reads Secure only on the measures of its step, grid metals refining, where South Korea has no measurable position (0). [2, 3]

### Medium fence — where industrial capability matters

This is where South Korea makes its real industrial choices: is a capability worth having (Strategic Industrial Value), and how strong is South Korea today (Competitive Position)?

**Preserve.** Every Preserve call rests on a measured position, and the battery and vehicle positions weigh most. Battery cells and packs, EV drivetrain components and EV and charger manufacturing combine top-quarter positions (82, 90 and 85) with high value and high confidence. Battery components reach the top quarter too (76), on four anchors from operating capacity to committed investment, with moderate confidence. PV modules and inverters (84) and transformers, switchgear and cables (89) also read Preserve, on moderate evidence. Inside the Watch step wafers and PV cells, the cell layers read Preserve, and the AIB gives South Korea a core seat in solar manufacturing. Reactors and steam generators sit exactly on the top-quarter bar, a narrow lead on trade baskets that mix reactor lines with generic boiler and steam-turbine parts, so the position may be overstated. [2, 3]

**Catch Up.** The strongest case is battery materials refining. It reads a Competitive Position of 0, yet South Korea holds 3% of world exports: the zero understates a real industry. Nuclear fuel and reactor parts (71) and blades, towers and nacelle parts (45) rest on shares of world supply and exports; in wind towers the AIB counts South Korea a supplier and the second-placed host for new allied capacity. [2, 3]

Other Catch Up calls should not be built on. Gas turbines and LNG plant (59) and drilling rigs, tankers and oil equipment (52) rest on what looks like a shared export line, and gas-turbine and drilling components partly on the gas plant step above it. They are allied supply roles more than national gaps. Wind turbines take their anchor from cables, transformers and switchgear the step's trade data share with the grid, not from turbines. Battery recycling rests on exports of battery waste and scrap, which show where scrap goes, not recycling capacity. [2, 3]

**Partner.** For minerals, rare-earth and wind materials and drilling and pump components, need is high but South Korea has little to build on. Dependence should be deliberate and spread across trusted producers: the United States, Canada and Australia for battery and EV minerals and Germany, Belgium and Spain for rare-earth and wind materials. Drilling and pump components can come from the United States, Canada and the Netherlands. In rare-earth and wind materials refining, allied supply falls short, so a purchase-only strategy will not hold. Grid components read Preserve on a position of 88, but the call is flagged for review by the engine, so this brief does not build on it. [1, 2, 3]

### Low fence — what not to prioritize

A credible strategy also says what not to localize. Watch is not a failure: it means affordable, diversified access matters more than domestic production.

- **Oil refining and gas liquids processing.** Oil refining is an established export business, with 6% of world supply; it needs no fence. [2, 3]
- **Polysilicon refining.** The solar-grade polysilicon layer reads Catch Up, but only on its step's export share, and South Korea's position in the step is 0. Treat that call as thin rather than a priority; the rest of the step stays open to diversified trusted producers. [2, 3]
- **Uranium, copper and bauxite ores.** Commodity imports, best bought from a diversified base.

## 3. South Korea's allied bargain

**What South Korea contributes.** In the Allied Industrial Base analysis, which protects the demand of a trusted network of 64 economies, South Korea is a core member of the trusted network. It is a Scale Manufacturing & Integration Platform with specialized technology role, and it ranks first among allies on outward cleantech-manufacturing FDI. It holds 36% of world exports or production of cathode materials and precursors, 17% of integrated circuits and 16% of accumulator parts and separators. It is the largest allied holder in battery components, with Japan and Poland the next holders, and in gas turbines and LNG plant, ahead of Germany and the United States. It is the best-placed trusted partner of 29 economies for gas turbines and LNG plant, 27 for battery components and 16 for battery materials refining. It holds a role in all 10 strategic compacts and a core seat in Battery Materials & Cells, Semiconductors & Power Electronics and Solar Manufacturing. [1, 2, 3]

**What South Korea needs.** Minerals, refined rare earths, enriched uranium and fuels from trusted producers. If China's supply stopped, as in the Decoupling scenario, South Korea would lose US$38.8 billion a year of imports across the Allied Industrial Base basket. New capacity at the best-placed hosts, led by Singapore, would restore about US$22.7 billion of it a year. Under Managed Interdependence, the default scenario, South Korea sits in the minimum coalition of five Sectoral Compacts (a different grouping from the strategic compacts above); under Decoupling it sits in 14 and is a demand anchor in 13 of them. [1, 2]

**The dependencies to make intentional.** These bargains turn that exchange into a specialization partners can count on.

## 4. Development strategy — build trusted capacity by helping partners Catch Up

Development cooperation can be the development arm of that bargain. A partnership needs evidence on both sides. On the Korean side, a step needs more trusted capacity: it is Partner, or Secure with allied delivery, its need is high, allied supply falls short, or allies need new capacity there. On the partner's side, a developing economy's same step is a credible Catch Up, inside the AIB trust screen.

South Korea's own supply chain drives most of the pairs. Of 47 pairs, 31, with 16 developing partners across six steps, rest on Korean need: rare-earth and wind materials refining; battery materials refining; EV drivetrain components; battery components; battery cells and packs; and blades, towers and nacelle parts. Another 16 rest on the allied gap alone. The table takes the leading partner of the highest-ranked steps, each partner once. Indonesia, Malaysia and Mexico are upper-middle-income economies, so finance, guarantees and offtake should lead. Viet Nam and the Philippines, both lower middle income, need groundwork first: project preparation, industrial infrastructure and skills. [1, 3, 5]

Battery materials refining is among the widest strands, level with battery and EV drivetrain components. 7 developing economies, led by the Philippines and Madagascar, have credible refining pathways; Mozambique, Colombia and Tanzania are review cases, their positions resting on mine and smelter capacity. For rare-earth and battery-metal refining, the Secure step of the rare-earth chain, no credible development-led supply solution is identified. Malaysia and Thailand host its new capacity in the AIB, a commercial route. In all, 33 pairs are commercial: the partner already Preserves the step or hosts its capacity, so trade, offtake and investment fit better than aid. [1, 3, 5]

Today's aid follows this map only in part; the data cover energy, industry and mining aid only. Aid coded to a partnership's technology reaches only Viet Nam and Malaysia. At some of their steps these partners are development partners; at others they are already on the commercial route, where trade and investment could take its place. Of the partners, 6 receive it at other steps and 3 at no supply-chain step; a partner with several partnership steps can appear in more than one group. The largest recipient, Ecuador, received $165 million over 2020 to 2024 and has no partnership here: that aid serves other aims, which this brief does not judge. [5]

## 5. Energy-system priorities

The energy system needs some technologies faster regardless of where the equipment is made. Nuclear power, EV adoption, solar and wind power clear the Accelerate bar of 60. The national target of 100 GW of renewables by 2030 compares with 37 GW installed in 2025, and at the recent build rate the shortfall is about 45 GW. Between 2020 and 2025, solar capacity grew from 14.8 to 30.4 GW and wind only from 1.6 to 2.4 GW. [3, 11]

High deployment priority does not imply high manufacturing priority. South Korea makes much of its nuclear and EV equipment, but the wind and solar build-out depends on buying turbines, modules and inverters competitively. Geothermal power and heat also clears the bar, driven by system need with moderate confidence; it needs confirming before it becomes a priority. Battery storage (56) and grid networks (56) sit just below the bar. [3, 11, 12]

## 7. Strategic agenda — what should South Korea do next?

### 1. Secure fuels and nuclear fuel through trusted contracts and stocks

Hold fuel stocks sized to the rebuild time, with long-term and capacity-reservation contracts across trusted producers. Supply the national-security end uses of uranium conversion and enrichment from trusted sources only, while the civilian market stays open. Build trusted capacity with partners through long-term contracts, co-investment and shared stockpiles with trusted processors such as Canada, the United Kingdom and France. [1, 2, 3]

### 2. Secure the rare-earth chain with allied refiners

Build diversified, qualified supply with trusted refiners such as the United States, Australia and Japan through offtake, public finance and allied agreements, hold stockpiles until it qualifies, and invest in recycling. No developing partner qualifies at rare-earth and battery-metal refining; Viet Nam qualifies at rare-earth and wind materials refining, the step that holds the rare-earth separation layer. [1, 2, 3, 5]

### 3. Keep the battery chain at the frontier and close the materials gap

Preserve cells, packs and components through next-generation research, workforce and supplier development, and finance for expansion tied to benchmarks. In battery materials, use time-limited production support, first-of-a-kind finance and demand creation, all tied to benchmarks, and keep the gate open to joint ventures that transfer capability. This is where the national task and the allied role coincide.

### 4. Make the equipment bargain explicit

Offer allies trusted battery materials and components, solar cells, transformers and gas plant through offtake and co-investment, and host the new power-cable capacity allies need. Secure minerals in return through offtake and co-investment with the United States, Canada and Australia. [1, 2, 3]

### 5. Build trusted battery and drivetrain capacity with developing partners

Start with the highest-ranked pairs, Viet Nam in rare-earth and wind materials refining and the Philippines in battery materials refining, where groundwork comes first. Also start with Indonesia in EV drivetrain components, Malaysia in battery cells and packs and Mexico in battery components, where Korean firms already invest. Use project preparation, standards and skills where the constraint is a public good, and development finance, guarantees and offtake where it is bankability. Move aid that still reaches commercial-route partners toward partners that need groundwork. [1, 3, 5]

### 6. Accelerate nuclear, solar and wind while keeping sourcing open

Close the deployment-policy gap for solar and wind, and keep nuclear and EV adoption on pace, through infrastructure, permitting and procurement. Buy turbines, modules and commodity hardware competitively from trusted suppliers, and attach trusted-supplier and security conditions to public deployment support and to equipment with built-in control.

## Appendix — evidence and method

**How this brief is built.** Every number comes from the Country page data of the Allied Industrial Base Explorer (the data release of 26 November 2026) and from the Allied Industrial Base analysis. Each technology is split into supply-chain stages, from extraction to recycling, and each step gets a strategic choice by fixed rules applied in order: [3]

- Secure where an acute vulnerability is measured;
- at deployment, Accelerate where deployment need reaches 60; [3]
- Watch where the step is a large, commoditized yard;
- Preserve where Competitive Position is in the top quarter (75) and either Strategic Industrial Value (bar 60) or supply-chain need (bar 67) is high; [3]
- Catch Up where the value is high, the position is not, and a capability anchor is corroborated by other evidence;
- Partner where supply-chain need is high but there is neither a top-quarter position nor, for a high-value step, a credible pathway;
- Watch otherwise.

Named layers inside a step, such as control software or magnets, take their own choice, and a step whose parts sit behind different fences is mixed. A value that rests on market size alone is capped just under the bar. Observed policy never decides a choice. The high fence (Secure) protects what cannot be allowed to fail; the medium fence covers capabilities worth building, keeping or sourcing deliberately and deployment to accelerate; the low fence (Watch) leaves a step open to competitive purchase.

**Review cases.** Some calls rest on low confidence, an engine review flag, a thin capability anchor, a known data artifact or, at deployment, a single indicator of low confidence. They stay on the map but never enter the Bottom line, the Agenda, the policy table or a headline mark. Of South Korea's steps, three are review cases. [3]

**Evidence and caveats.** The battery materials Catch Up has high confidence. Its Competitive Position of 0 sits beside an AIB share of 36% in cathode materials: the position measure gives South Korea nothing for plant capacity or investment, while exports and the AIB show a large industry. The wind-turbine Catch Up is anchored on trade codes the step shares with the grid (cables, transformers and switchgear), and the battery-recycling Catch Up on exports of battery waste and scrap; neither measures the capability its name implies. Gas turbines and LNG plant and drilling rigs, tankers and oil equipment show nearly the same share of world exports (11% and 11%), which suggests overlapping trade codes. The oil step's value of 60 only just reaches the bar. Reactors sit exactly on the top-quarter line. The uranium Secure call is a dual-use call, with the fence on the national-security end uses; coercive suppliers provide 50% of South Korea's imports. Grain-oriented electrical steel takes its Secure call from the measures of grid metals refining. Coal-plant components read Preserve and coal-plant equipment Catch Up on real export positions, though coal power is a Watch. In all, 18 steps lack the data for a call, including all of geothermal and green hydrogen manufacturing and most recycling. Recorded policy ranks clean hydrogen use and electrolyser systems among South Korea's highest priorities (98 and 95 out of a hundred). But deployment need rests on a single indicator and the manufacturing steps cannot be assessed, so this brief does not settle that disagreement. Competitive Position ranks South Korea among economies that already supply a step, so a zero means no measurable position. Partner lists name where allied capacity sits, which can include trading hubs, not who owns it. [1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12, 13, 14]

**Development partnerships.** A pair joins a Korean step with a strategic reason for more allied capacity and a developing economy (not high income) whose same step is a Catch Up. The partner needs an anchor corroborated beyond context signals, sufficient evidence and confidence, and a place inside the AIB trust screen; on a step whose data describe different products, the anchor must measure the product the need is about. A partner that already hosts the step's new capacity in the AIB, or Preserves it, is a commercial case. Aid comes from the OECD Creditor Reporting System by purpose code. Components and Assembly share codes, no code funds recycling, and many codes name a stage rather than a technology, so aid at a step is not aid for that technology. Aid is called technology-coded only where codes naming the technology hold most of it. A constraint on a partner is named only where the data observe its input; otherwise the table says the data do not cover it. In the relationship table an observed zero is stated as nothing recorded, data that do not cover a pair are left out, and the direct investment position is economy-wide, not tied to the step. Export-credit finance is reported apart and never counted as aid, and direct investment uses the IMF positions (the partner's reports where South Korea reports none). Development Potential, an older partner score, is a supporting indicator only. The full list of pairs, with every field, is in the development-partnership export.
