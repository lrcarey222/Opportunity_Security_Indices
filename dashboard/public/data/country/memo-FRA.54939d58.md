# France and the Allied Industrial Base

*What to secure, build, source from partners, and deploy*

*Generated from Allied Industrial Base Explorer data; evidence, sources and caveats in the appendix. Data as of 26 November 2026.*

## 1. Bottom line — France's strategic role

France's distinctive role in the Allied Industrial Base is as its nuclear specialist and a broad, mid-sized equipment maker. Allies rely on it for nuclear reactors and enrichment machinery, 30% of world exports or production, and it holds core seats in Nuclear Fuel & Reactor Systems and Electric Vehicles. Elsewhere its roles are substitutable, and the AIB assigns it no primary strategic role at its current thresholds. Its exposure is imported fuel, the rare-earth chain and the control of imported equipment: oil and gas, rare-earth refining and magnets, grid and battery software and transformer steel sit behind the High fence. Its industrial effort should keep enrichment, nuclear parts and reactors, wind parts and battery components competitive and catch up in EVs, drivetrains and battery cells, where allies also need new capacity. Most of its manufacturing positions are a few percent of world supply, so catching up should go with allied makers. The nuclear manufacturing calls need care, because their trade baskets mix reactor parts with boiler and steam-turbine parts; the AIB's reactor measure is the firmer sign. At home only EV adoption needs a faster build-out, and France's own need gives its development cooperation a narrow base, battery cells. [1, 2]

- **Secure.** Oil and gas supply, which takes one to three years to rebuild; potentially coercive suppliers provide 52% of oil imports. The rare-earth chain, where China holds 94% of world refining supply, and the magnets of EV motors and wind generators. The control software of battery, grid and inverter equipment, and grain-oriented electrical steel for transformers. The answer is trusted contracts, stocks, qualified suppliers and control of command paths, not self-sufficiency. [2, 3]
- **Build / preserve.** Preserve uranium enrichment (38% of world exports of enriched uranium), nuclear fuel and reactor parts, reactors, wind parts and battery components. Catch Up in EVs, EV drivetrain components and battery cells and packs, with allied makers where the base is thin. Judge the nuclear parts and reactor calls on the AIB's reactor measures, since their baskets mix in boiler parts. [2]
- **Partner.** Battery and EV minerals from trusted producers such as Canada, the United States and Australia; allied supply covers the need. With developing partners, add trusted battery-cell capacity through development finance and offtake. [1, 2, 4]
- **Deploy / stay open.** Accelerate EV adoption and charging, buying equipment competitively from trusted suppliers, with security conditions on connected equipment. Leave open wafers and PV cells, polysilicon, plant equipment for gas, oil and coal, and uranium ore, bought from a diversified base of trusted producers.

## 2. Where France should secure, compete and stay open

### High fence — what cannot fail

Secure covers three whole steps and nine layers inside other steps, grouped into five capabilities, all in the table with oil and gas together. Secure means surviving a prolonged cutoff or a hostile command through trusted supply, stocks, redundancy or control of the command path, not making everything at home. [4]

Turbine, vehicle, reactor and oil and gas network controls read Watch for France. The wind magnets read Secure although their step, blades, towers and nacelle parts, reads Preserve on its own position. [4]

### Medium fence — where industrial capability matters

This is where France makes its real industrial choices: is a capability worth having (Strategic Industrial Value), and how strong is France today (Competitive Position)? Most French positions rest on world supply shares of a few percent, ranking high only among the economies that already make them.

**Preserve.** Uranium conversion and enrichment reads Preserve on a position of 95. Its measured anchor is exports of enriched uranium, 38% of the world's. Its supply share of 25% counts uranium compounds, yellowcake included, and converted uranium trades under the mining step's codes, so the data see enrichment, not conversion. With Russia holding 22% of that compound-based supply, trusted enrichment matters to allies. Nuclear fuel and reactor parts (80) and reactors and steam generators (87) also read Preserve, but their trade baskets mix reactor parts with generic boiler and steam-turbine parts. The AIB's own measures are the firmer sign of a nuclear supplier: 30% of world exports or production of reactors and enrichment machinery and 16% of fuel assemblies and zirconium. Wind turbines (87) and blades, towers and nacelle parts (86) rest on operating capacity, but on 2% of world supply each, and the turbine step's trade codes are mostly cables, transformers and switchgear. Battery components (82) rest on three anchors with moderate confidence. PV modules and inverters read Preserve only on low confidence, a review case. [1, 2, 4]

**Catch Up.** EV and charger manufacturing (68) and EV drivetrain components (68) combine high value with high confidence; vehicles rest on three anchors, operating capacity among them. Battery cells and packs add high need (77) but rest on a single anchor, 1% of world supply, while China provides 57% of imports. [2, 4]

Other Catch Up calls are weaker than they look, and this brief does not build on them:

- **Battery materials refining:** a position of 0 on a single export share (2%). It shows China with 94% of world supply, the share of the Secure rare-earth refining step, which suggests overlapping trade codes. [2, 4]
- **Battery recycling:** a single export share (3%) in trade codes for battery waste, scrap and residues. [2]
- **Rare-earth and wind materials refining:** low confidence, a review case, as is transformers, switchgear and cables (low confidence). [4]

**Partner.** France holds 0% of world supply in battery and EV minerals and no pathway to scale it, so it should depend deliberately on trusted producers such as Canada, the United States and Australia; allied supply covers the need. Its largest supplier today, Gabon, a member of the trusted network, is not among them. Waste-oil recovery reads Partner on low confidence, a review case. [1, 2, 4]

### Low fence — what not to prioritize

A credible strategy also says what not to localize. Watch is not a failure: it means affordable, diversified access matters more than domestic production.

- **Wafers and PV cells, and polysilicon refining.** Need for wafers and cells is low (18), their trade codes include chip-industry codes, and their cell layers read Catch Up only on inherited anchors. France has no measurable position in polysilicon refining (0), and its solar-grade layer's Catch Up rests on the step's export share alone. [2, 4]
- **Gas, oil and coal plant equipment.** Need is low, value below the bar and positions below the top quarter. Yet 7 economies count France as their best-placed trusted partner for coal-plant equipment, where it holds 9% of world supply: a supplier role to keep reliable through competition, not a fence. [2, 4]
- **Uranium mining.** Need is very high (99), and Kazakhstan, the largest potentially coercive supplier, provides 34% of imports. Yet the step reads Watch, a large, commoditized yard where France has no mines: buy from a diversified base of trusted producers. [2, 4]

## 3. France's allied bargain

**What France contributes.** France is a core member of the trusted network, the 64 economies the Allied Industrial Base treats as allied. The analysis assigns it no primary strategic role at its current thresholds. It holds a role in eight of the ten strategic compacts, most of them substitutable. Its supply is essential where it holds core seats, in Nuclear Fuel & Reactor Systems and Electric Vehicles: 30% of world exports or production of nuclear reactors and enrichment machinery and 10% of electric buses, trucks and tractors. It also supplies 16% of fuel assemblies and zirconium and 12% of uranium conversion and enrichment products, natural uranium compounds included. 8 economies count France as their best-placed trusted partner somewhere in the chain, 7 of them for coal-plant equipment. [1, 2, 4]

**What France needs.** Fuel, minerals, magnets and battery cells. If China's supply stopped, as in the Decoupling scenario, France would lose US$8.8 billion a year of imports across the Allied Industrial Base basket. New capacity at the best-placed hosts, led by Germany, would restore only about US$2.8 billion of it a year. Under Managed Interdependence, the default scenario, France sits in the minimum coalition of two Sectoral Compacts (the finer units beneath the strategic compacts); under Decoupling, in three. [1, 2]

**The dependencies to make intentional.** These bargains turn that exchange into a specialization partners can count on.

## 5. Energy-system priorities

Wherever the equipment is made, only EV adoption and charging clears the Accelerate bar of 60 on settled evidence: a need of 64 on two measures with moderate confidence. Clean hydrogen reads higher, but on a single indicator of low confidence, so it is a review case. Nuclear power sits just below the bar (56), though demand adequacy alone (62) clears it and capacity was flat (61.4 to 61.4 GW); it deserves the next review. Renewables are close to target: 91 GW by 2030 against 84 GW in 2025, a shortfall of about 3 GW at the recent build rate. Storage, solar, wind and grid networks are worth watching, not forcing. [4, 11, 12]

## 7. Strategic agenda — what should France do next?

### 1. Secure fuel, the rare-earth chain and the grid's command layer

Size oil and gas stocks to the rebuild time. Reserve capacity with trusted producers such as Canada, Norway and the United States for oil and Norway, Australia and Canada for gas; partner evidence is weak, so test the terms. Qualify rare-earth metal and magnet supply from allies such as Australia, the United States and Japan through offtake and co-investment, with stockpiles until it qualifies. Apply trusted-source and certification rules to battery, inverter, metering and grid-control software, and reserve transformer steel with trusted steelmakers. [1, 2, 4]

### 2. Keep enrichment and reactor supply competitive for allies, and diversify uranium

Keep enrichment, the nuclear position the data measure most directly, at the frontier with public finance and allied investment tied to benchmarks, so allies can move away from Russia's supply. Hold reactor and fuel-assembly supply with R&D and workforce and supplier development. Buy uranium from diversified trusted producers. [2]

### 3. Catch up in EVs, drivetrains and battery cells, with support tied to benchmarks

Tie production incentives, workforce development and demand creation to benchmarks, with sunsets. Build at home where the base is established and with allied makers such as Germany, the United States, Japan and Spain for the rest. [1]

### 4. Build trusted battery-cell capacity with developing partners

Start with Malaysia, the own-need partner, checking first why the capacity data show no plant; lead with development finance, guarantees and offtake. Pursue allied-gap pairs, such as Viet Nam in EV and charger manufacturing, jointly with allies. [1, 4, 5]

### 5. Accelerate EV adoption while keeping sourcing open

Deploy faster through charging infrastructure, permitting and procurement, buying from trusted suppliers with security conditions on connected equipment. Keep local-content protection on storage equipment time-limited and tied to Catch Up benchmarks, with sunsets, and hold wind equipment to targeted remedies and conditional procurement.

## Appendix — evidence and method

**How this brief is built.** Every number comes from the Country page data of the Allied Industrial Base Explorer (the data release of 26 November 2026) and from the Allied Industrial Base analysis. Each technology is split into supply-chain stages from extraction to recycling, and each step gets a strategic choice by fixed rules applied in order: [4]

- Secure where an acute vulnerability is measured;
- at deployment, Accelerate where deployment need reaches 60; [4]
- Watch where the step is a large, commoditized yard;
- Preserve where Competitive Position is in the top quarter (75) and either Strategic Industrial Value (bar 60) or supply-chain need (bar 67) is high; [4]
- Catch Up where the value is high, the position is not, and a capability anchor is corroborated by other evidence;
- Partner where supply-chain need is high but there is neither a top-quarter position nor, for a high-value step, a credible pathway;
- Watch otherwise.

Named layers inside a step, such as control software or magnets, take their own choice, and a step whose parts sit behind different fences is mixed. A value that rests on market size alone is capped just under the bar. Observed policy never decides a choice. The high fence (Secure) protects what cannot be allowed to fail; the medium fence covers capabilities worth building, keeping or sourcing deliberately and deployment to accelerate; the low fence (Watch) leaves a step open to competitive purchase.

**Evidence and caveats.** The oil and gas calls rest on measured import dependence and supplier concentration, and the EV and battery-cell Catch Up calls have high confidence. The enrichment Preserve rests on exports of enriched uranium; its supply share of 25% is a share in uranium compounds, yellowcake included, and the AIB's measure of 12% also covers natural uranium compounds. Natural uranium hexafluoride, the output of conversion, trades with yellowcake under the mining step's codes, so conversion has no measure of its own. The baskets of nuclear fuel and reactor parts and of reactors mix reactor parts with generic boiler and steam-turbine parts, the kind of equipment that also fills the coal-plant steps. Their Preserve calls therefore lean on the AIB's reactor and fuel-assembly measures. The wind turbines step's trade codes are mostly cables, transformers and switchgear; battery recycling's are battery waste, scrap and residues; rare-earth and wind materials refining's are mostly copper wire rod by value. The solar wafer and module baskets include chip-industry codes, and the Catch Up layers inside the Watch solar steps inherit their steps' anchors. Rare-earth and battery-metal refining and battery materials refining show the identical China share of world supply, which suggests overlapping trade codes. Grain-oriented electrical steel reads Secure on the measures of its step, which cover grid metals as a whole; sintered magnets read Secure in both EV drivetrains and wind components, behind the Secure rare-earth refining step. Uranium mining reads Watch as a large yard despite a need of 99. 18 steps lack the data for a call, including geothermal supply, green hydrogen manufacturing and most recycling. Recorded policy ranks electrolyser systems (96) and stacks (83) near the top of France's own measures, but those steps cannot be assessed and hydrogen deployment need rests on a single indicator, so this brief does not settle that disagreement. Import shares follow the trading partner, so a hub such as Belgium for gas can stand for the producers behind it. Competitive Position ranks France among economies that already supply a step, so a zero means no measurable position. Partner lists name where allied capacity sits, which can include trading hubs, not who owns it. [1, 2, 4, 13, 14]

**Development partnerships.** A pair joins a French step with a strategic reason for more allied capacity and a developing economy (not high income) whose same step is a Catch Up. The reason is France's own need, or the allied gap alone, where allies need new capacity, which is the same for every donor in scope. The partner needs an anchor corroborated beyond context signals, sufficient evidence and confidence, and a place inside the AIB trust screen; on a step whose data describe different products, the anchor must measure the product the need is about. A partner that already hosts the step's new capacity in the AIB, or Preserves it, is a commercial case. Aid comes from the OECD Creditor Reporting System by purpose code and covers energy, industry and mining aid only. Components and Assembly share codes, no code funds recycling, and many codes name a stage rather than a technology, so aid at a step is not aid for that technology. Aid is called technology-coded only where codes naming the technology hold at least half of it, and a partner with several steps can sit in several aid groups. A constraint on a partner is named only where the data observe its input; otherwise the table says the data do not cover it. In the relationship table an observed zero is stated as nothing recorded, data that do not cover a pair are left out, and the direct investment position is economy-wide, not tied to the step. Export-credit finance is reported apart and never counted as aid, and direct investment uses the IMF positions (the partner's reports where France reports none).
