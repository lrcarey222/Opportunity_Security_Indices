## Bottom line — South Korea's strategic role

South Korea's distinctive role in the Allied Industrial Base is as its battery maker and equipment builder. It holds {{aib.contributes.cathode-materials-and-precursors.share}} of world exports or production of cathode materials and precursors. It is the largest allied holder of {{aib.division.batteries-components.name}} and of {{aib.division.gas-assembly.name}}, and it is the best-placed trusted partner of {{aib.recommended_economies}} economies for at least one step. Nationally, that strength reads Preserve in battery cells, EV drivetrains and vehicles, reactors and gas plant. In battery components and materials it reads only Catch Up: South Korea is the largest allied holder of components and a leading cathode supplier, but its national position sits below the top quarter of producers. Its exposure lies at the start of the chain. It is among the most import-dependent economies for oil, gas and coal, relies on {{cells.electric-vehicles-refining.coercive_top}} for refined rare earths, and buys {{cells.nuclear-refining.top_supplier_share}} of its imported uranium conversion and enrichment from {{cells.nuclear-refining.top_supplier}}. For minerals, rare-earth and wind materials and grid components it should depend deliberately on trusted producers. At home, the energy system needs nuclear, solar and wind power and electric vehicles faster. Its development cooperation can follow its firms' investment abroad, helping partners add trusted battery and drivetrain capacity.

### Low fence — what not to prioritize

A credible strategy also says what not to localize. Watch is not a failure: it means affordable, diversified access matters more than domestic production.

- **Oil refining and gas liquids processing.** Oil refining is an established export business, with {{cells.oil-refining.share}} of world supply; it needs no fence.
- **Polysilicon refining.** The solar-grade polysilicon layer reads Catch Up, but only on its step's export share, and South Korea's position in the step is {{cells.solar-refining.cp}}. Treat that call as thin rather than a priority; the rest of the step stays open to diversified trusted producers.
- **Uranium, copper and bauxite ores.** Commodity imports, best bought from a diversified base.

## Strategic agenda — what should South Korea do next?

### 1. Secure fuels and nuclear fuel through trusted contracts and stocks

Hold fuel stocks sized to the rebuild time, with long-term and capacity-reservation contracts across trusted producers. Supply the national-security end uses of uranium conversion and enrichment from trusted sources only, while the civilian market stays open. Build trusted capacity with partners through long-term contracts, co-investment and shared stockpiles with trusted processors such as {{cells.nuclear-refining.partners}}.

### 2. Secure the rare-earth chain with allied refiners

Build diversified, qualified supply with trusted refiners such as {{cells.electric-vehicles-refining.partners}} through offtake, public finance and allied agreements, hold stockpiles until it qualifies, and invest in recycling. No developing partner qualifies at rare-earth and battery-metal refining; {{dev.rows.r1.partner}} qualifies at {{dev.rows.r1.capability}}, the step that holds the rare-earth separation layer.

### 3. Keep the battery chain at the frontier and close the materials gap

Preserve cells and packs through next-generation research, workforce and supplier development, and finance for expansion tied to benchmarks. In battery components and materials, use time-limited production support, first-of-a-kind finance and demand creation, all tied to benchmarks, and keep the gate open to joint ventures that transfer capability. This is where the national task and the allied role coincide.

### 4. Make the equipment bargain explicit

Offer allies trusted battery materials and components, electric rail vehicles, transformers and gas plant through offtake and co-investment, and secure minerals in return through offtake and co-investment with {{cells.batteries-extraction.partners}}. Protection of grid components should give way to partnership terms.

### 5. Build trusted battery and drivetrain capacity with developing partners

Start with the highest-ranked pairs, {{dev.rows.r1.partner}} in {{dev.rows.r1.capability}} and {{dev.rows.r2.partner}} in {{dev.rows.r2.capability}}, where groundwork comes first. Also start with {{dev.rows.r3.partner}} in {{dev.rows.r3.capability}}, {{dev.rows.r4.partner}} in {{dev.rows.r4.capability}} and {{dev.rows.r5.partner}} in {{dev.rows.r5.capability}}, where Korean firms already invest. Use project preparation, standards and skills where the constraint is a public good, and development finance, guarantees and offtake where it is bankability. Move aid that still reaches commercial-route partners toward partners that need groundwork.

### 6. Accelerate nuclear, solar and wind while keeping sourcing open

Close the deployment-policy gap for solar and wind, and keep nuclear and EV adoption on pace, through infrastructure, permitting and procurement. Buy turbines, modules and commodity hardware competitively from trusted suppliers, and attach trusted-supplier and security conditions to public deployment support and to equipment with built-in control.

## Appendix — evidence and method

**How this brief is built.** Every number comes from the Country page data of the Allied Industrial Base Explorer (the data release of {{meta.as_of_label}}) and from the Allied Industrial Base analysis. Each technology is split into supply-chain stages, from extraction to recycling, and each step gets a strategic choice by fixed rules applied in order:

- Secure where an acute vulnerability is measured;
- at deployment, Accelerate where deployment need reaches {{meta.bars.deployment}};
- Watch where the step is a large, commoditized yard;
- Preserve where Competitive Position is in the top quarter ({{meta.bars.cp}}) and either Strategic Industrial Value (bar {{meta.bars.prize}}) or supply-chain need (bar {{meta.bars.need}}) is high;
- Catch Up where the value is high, the position is not, and a capability anchor is corroborated by other evidence;
- Partner where supply-chain need is high but there is neither a top-quarter position nor, for a high-value step, a credible pathway;
- Watch otherwise.

Named layers inside a step, such as control software or magnets, take their own choice, and a step whose parts sit behind different fences is mixed. A value that rests on market size alone is capped just under the bar. Observed policy never decides a choice. The high fence (Secure) protects what cannot be allowed to fail; the medium fence covers capabilities worth building, keeping or sourcing deliberately and deployment to accelerate; the low fence (Watch) leaves a step open to competitive purchase.

**Review cases.** Some calls rest on low confidence, an engine review flag, a thin capability anchor, a known data artifact or, at deployment, a single indicator of low confidence. They stay on the map but never enter the Bottom line, the Agenda, the policy table or a headline mark. Of South Korea's steps, {{counts.review|nw}} are review cases.
