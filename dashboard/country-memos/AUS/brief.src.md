## Bottom line — Australia's strategic role

Australia's distinctive role in the Allied Industrial Base is as its trusted supplier of critical minerals: it holds the largest allied position in battery and EV minerals, and {{aib.recommended_by.batteries-extraction.n}} economies have it as their best-placed trusted partner for battery minerals. That is an allied role first: nationally, those two mining steps read Watch and a low-confidence Preserve. Its domestic industrial effort should go one step down from the mine, into battery materials refining and the rare-earth chain, where it has operating capacity and committed investment to build on. For battery cells, EV drivetrains, wind turbines and solar inverters it should depend deliberately on trusted producers, which hold more capacity than Australia though not yet enough. Its development cooperation can help developing partners with credible pathways add that capacity. The larger domestic task is deployment: storage, grid, wind and solar all need to be built faster, and buying equipment competitively serves that build-out better than localizing it. The fence that matters is narrow but high: the rare-earth chain, and the control software that can command Australia's grid, batteries, inverters and turbines.

:::choices
- **Secure.** The rare-earth chain from separation to magnets, where {{cells.electric-vehicles-refining.coercive_top}} holds {{cells.electric-vehicles-refining.coercive_top_share}} of world refining supply and trusted suppliers only {{cells.electric-vehicles-refining.trusted_share}}; the control software of grid equipment, inverters, batteries and turbines; and the control systems of gas and oil networks. The answer is qualified trusted supply and control of command paths, not self-sufficiency.
- **Build / preserve.** Catch Up in battery materials refining (lithium chemicals, cathode and anode), the best-anchored case for building. Build rare-earth separation and metal-making on the mining base. Keep mineral supply reliable for the allies that depend on it: an allied commitment, not a domestic fence.
- **Partner.** Battery cells, packs, components and recycling, EV drivetrains and vehicles, wind turbines and their parts, solar inverters, and solar-grade polysilicon. Source them from trusted producers, such as {{cells.batteries-components.partners}} for battery components and {{cells.wind-components.partners}} for wind parts. Where allied supply falls short, add trusted capacity through co-investment and, with developing partners, development finance and offtake.
- **Deploy / stay open.** Accelerate battery storage, grid networks, wind and solar power. Buy commodity hardware competitively from trusted suppliers, with security conditions on equipment that carries built-in control. Leave open reactors, nuclear fuel and reactor parts, wafers and PV cells, and gas and oil plant equipment.

Secure does not mean making everything at home. It means being able to survive a prolonged cutoff or a hostile command, through qualified trusted supply, stocks, redundancy or control of the command path. ### Medium fence — where industrial capability matters

This is where Australia makes its real industrial choices. Two questions decide each step: is the capability worth having (Strategic Industrial Value), and how strong is Australia today (Competitive Position)?

**Preserve.** No Preserve call clears the evidence bar. {{cells.electric-vehicles-extraction.name|cap}} reads Preserve on a top-quarter Competitive Position, but the call is {{cells.electric-vehicles-extraction.review}}, and battery mineral mining reads Watch. The table below treats them as a single capability: the national call stays open, while allies rely on Australia as their largest holder. What keeps it strategic is reliability and the processing that follows it, not a domestic fence.

**Catch Up.** {{cells.batteries-refining.name|cap}} is the clearest case for building. Its value is high and its position below the top quarter, and it rests on {{cells.batteries-refining.anchors|nw}} capability anchors, from operating capacity to committed investment. {{cells.batteries-refining.coercive_top}} holds {{cells.batteries-refining.coercive_top_share}} of world supply and provides {{cells.batteries-refining.china_share}} of Australia's imports, so trusted capacity here is scarce for every ally. Lithium hydroxide and carbonate, cathode and anode materials are all Catch Up. {{cells.wind-refining.name|cap}} and {{cells.nuclear-refining.name}} also read Catch Up, but they rest on {{cells.wind-refining.review}} and {{cells.nuclear-refining.review}}; both are review cases, listed in the appendix.

**Partner.** The equipment Australia installs is worth having, but Australia holds almost none of world supply in it and has no capability anchor to build from, so duplicating it would be slow and costly. Dependence should be deliberate and spread across several trusted producers: {{cells.batteries-assembly.partners}} for cells and packs, {{cells.electric-vehicles-components.partners}} for EV drivetrains, and {{cells.wind-components.partners}} for wind components. In {{cells.batteries-assembly.name}}, {{cells.electric-vehicles-components.name}}, {{cells.wind-assembly.name}}, and {{cells.solar-assembly.name}}, allied supply falls short, so a purchase-only strategy will not hold. Solar-grade polysilicon belongs here too: source it deliberately from trusted producers rather than build it.

### Low fence — what not to prioritize

A credible strategy also says what not to localize. Watch is not a failure: it means affordable, diversified access matters more than domestic production. Five cases stand out.

- **Wafers and PV cells, and silicon metal.** Solar is an Accelerate technology, so Australia needs the modules, but making wafers, cells and silicon metal at home adds little strategic value (solar-grade polysilicon, above, is a Partner case); recorded industrial-policy measures run ahead of that case.
- **Grid metals refining and grid components.** The grid must grow faster, but copper and aluminium processing and grid components are Watch steps with low supply-chain need, best bought competitively. The one Catch Up layer, grain-oriented electrical steel, inherits the step's grid-metal export anchor rather than any position in electrical steel; only grid control layers, such as control software, smart meters and inverter firmware, sit behind the High fence.
- **Nuclear fuel and reactor parts.** These carry a high value, but nuclear power is not an Australian deployment priority and the supply-chain need is low; keep access open through diversified imports and procurement, not an industry.

**What Australia contributes.** In the Allied Industrial Base analysis Australia is a {{aib.role}}, first among allies on {{aib.first_on}}. It holds {{aib.contributes.lithium-minerals.share}} of world exports or production of lithium minerals and {{aib.contributes.rare-earth-metals.share}} of rare-earth metals. ## Strategic agenda — what should Australia do next?

### 1. Secure the rare-earth chain through a mixed domestic and allied build-out

Scale separation and metal-making at home, where the anchors are, and build the step to magnets with allied partners through co-investment and offtake, holding stocks until trusted supply qualifies. This is the one place where a chokepoint meets an Australian capability. No developing partner qualifies for a development partnership in this step: the allied route runs through trusted refiners such as {{cells.electric-vehicles-refining.partners}}, and through {{dev.by_step.electric-vehicles-refining.hosts}}, which already host new capacity, on commercial terms.

### 2. Catch up in battery materials refining with time-limited support tied to benchmarks

Lithium chemicals, cathode and anode rest on the strongest anchors in the brief. Tie production support, first-of-a-kind finance, workforce development and demand creation to benchmarks, and keep the gate open to joint ventures on terms that transfer capability. Allies need new capacity here too: project preparation, industrial infrastructure and workforce development, then offtake at scale, with refiners such as {{dev.by_step.batteries-refining.development_top}} add trusted supply alongside Australia's own.

### 3. Keep mineral supply reliable as an allied commitment

The allied division of labor makes Australian battery and EV minerals a single point of failure. Reliability through price cycles, and the processing that follows, matter more than a domestic fence.

### 4. Make the equipment bargain explicit

Buy turbines, cells and inverters from several trusted producers, and co-invest where allied supply falls short. Recorded measures that localize these steps should give way to partnership terms.

### 5. Build trusted equipment capacity with developing partners through development finance and offtake

Start with {{dev.rows.r1.partner}} in {{dev.rows.r1.capability}}, {{dev.rows.r2.partner}} in {{dev.rows.r2.capability}} and {{dev.rows.r3.partner}} in {{dev.rows.r3.capability}}. Use project preparation, standards and skills where the constraint is a public good, and development finance, guarantees and offtake where it is bankability. The objective is both an additional trusted supplier and a durable manufacturing capability in the partner economy.

### 6. Accelerate storage, grid, wind and solar while keeping sourcing open

Deploy faster through infrastructure, permitting and procurement; buy commodity hardware competitively from trusted suppliers, and attach trusted-supplier and security conditions to public deployment support and to equipment with built-in control. Wafers, PV cells and reactor parts are not localization priorities: affordable, diversified access is.

## Appendix — evidence and method

**How this brief is built.** Every number comes from the Country page data of the Allied Industrial Base Explorer (the data release of {{meta.as_of_label}}) and from the Allied Industrial Base analysis. Each technology is split into six supply-chain stages, and each step gets a strategic choice by fixed rules applied in order:

- Secure where an acute vulnerability is measured;
- at deployment, Accelerate where deployment need reaches {{meta.bars.deployment}};
- Watch where the step is a large, commoditized yard;
- Preserve where Competitive Position is in the top quarter ({{meta.bars.cp}}) and either Strategic Industrial Value (bar {{meta.bars.prize}}) or supply-chain need (bar {{meta.bars.need}}) is high;
- Catch Up where the value is high, the position is not, and a capability anchor is corroborated by other evidence;
- Partner where supply-chain need is high but there is neither a top-quarter position nor, for a high-value step, a credible pathway;
- Watch otherwise.

Named layers inside a step, such as control software or magnets, take their own choice, and a step whose parts sit behind different fences is mixed. A value that rests on market size alone is capped just under the bar. Observed policy never decides a choice. The high fence (Secure) protects what cannot be allowed to fail; the medium fence covers capabilities worth building, keeping or sourcing deliberately and deployment to accelerate; the low fence (Watch) leaves a step open to competitive purchase.

**Review cases.** Some calls rest on low confidence, an engine review flag, a thin capability anchor, a known data artifact or, at deployment, a single indicator of low confidence. They stay on the map but never enter the Bottom line, the Agenda, the policy table or a headline mark. {{counts.review|cap}} steps are review cases.
